On Friday, 16 October 2026, China said it plans to <mark>restrict or ban exports of 40 technologies</mark> spanning artificial intelligence, chips and solar power. The Ministry of Commerce and the Ministry of Science and Technology jointly published a draft update to the country's catalogue of export-controlled technologies. A commerce ministry spokesperson called the move "necessary" and cited national security.

![Key figure card showing 40 technologies in China's draft export list](/images/2026-10-17-china-proposes-export-curbs-on-40-technologies-including-ai/key-figure.svg)

## What happened

The draft updates China's catalogue of technologies subject to export bans or restrictions. According to the Associated Press, it names <mark>23 AI technologies</mark>, 12 solar technologies and 5 biomedicine technologies. Several chip design technologies, including some for AI processing chips and memory chips, are also on the list.

On AI, the draft would prohibit exports of core technologies for large models trained on sensitive national data. It would also bar AI agents designed for highly sensitive settings, such as national defense and critical information infrastructure. Exports of AI models with <mark>2 trillion or more parameters</mark>, or with certain world-leading capabilities, could be restricted. Parameter count is a measure of a model's learning capacity. AP noted that Moonshot's Kimi K3 and Alibaba's Qwen 3.8-Max exceed 2 trillion parameters, but the proposal does not name specific models.

The 12 solar technologies include those for making polysilicon, a core raw material in the solar supply chain. The 5 biomedicine items include technologies related to cell and gene therapy.

![Bar chart of the 40 technologies: 23 AI, 12 solar, 5 biomedicine](/images/2026-10-17-china-proposes-export-curbs-on-40-technologies-including-ai/breakdown.svg)

The ministry said the measures aim to reinforce "the security bottom line", prevent misuse and abuse, and keep AI under human control. The reports do not give an effective date or say when the draft would be finalised.

## Background

The proposal follows months of reporting that Beijing was considering such steps. In July, The Next Web, citing the Financial Times, reported that the Ministry of Commerce was consulting major domestic AI and chip firms on tighter controls beyond existing curbs on rare earths and certain semiconductor materials. At that stage nothing had been decided and no draft had been published. The reported goal was to keep China's most advanced systems and fastest-growing startups from being acquired by Western entities.

The move also sits within a longer technology contest. <mark>U.S.-led restrictions</mark> have limited China's access to the most advanced AI chips and the machines that make them. In June, the U.S. Commerce Department asked Anthropic to bar foreign nationals from using its most powerful models, Fable 5 and Mythos 5, citing national security; AP reports those restrictions were later lifted.

![Timeline: June US request to Anthropic, July FT report, October draft](/images/2026-10-17-china-proposes-export-curbs-on-40-technologies-including-ai/timeline.svg)

The draft also arrives while the two countries are making progress on AI safety and governance dialogues, and on a notification mechanism for AI incidents that could threaten national security, AP reported.

## Why it matters

Models of this size are used well beyond China, and some Chinese models are freely downloadable. The Next Web, citing The Decoder, noted that European developers and smaller firms relying on such models could face narrower access if controls reach them. In the July reporting, officials were said to be considering that any curbs might apply only to future models.

Analysts read the draft as part of a wider fragmentation. Lian Jye Su, chief analyst at Omdia, said it "can be seen as a response to Western countries posting sovereignty requirements on AI" and other key technologies, and that the aim is to better regulate access to Chinese technologies. Gary Ng, senior economist at Natixis, said <mark>AI is increasingly strategic</mark> for both China and the U.S., adding that "the world is getting more fragmented in trade and investment" and in access to technology.

The solar items matter because polysilicon is a core input for the global solar supply chain, so any restriction on the know-how to produce it could affect manufacturers outside China.

## What to watch

- <mark>Final scope</mark>: whether the published draft is changed before adoption, and which models or chips are covered. Reports in July said thresholds and open-source handling were unclear.
- Effective date: none has been announced.
- Reactions from Washington and other governments, and from firms that use Chinese models or solar technology.
- Whether the AI safety dialogue between Beijing and Washington continues alongside the new controls.

## Editor's Insight

The draft turns a one-way tool into a two-way one. For years, US-led restrictions limited China's access to advanced AI chips and the machines that make them. Now Beijing is writing its own list, and its logic resembles Washington's: in June, the US Commerce Department asked Anthropic to bar foreign nationals from its most powerful models on national-security grounds, a step that was later lifted. Both governments now treat frontier AI models as strategic assets rather than ordinary products.

China's leverage sits in different places from America's. The draft targets know-how rather than finished goods, including the technology to make polysilicon, a core input for the global solar supply chain, and large models above 2 trillion parameters. Restricting the know-how protects China's lead in production while making it harder for manufacturers elsewhere to build their own supply. It also follows a clear progression from earlier curbs on rare earths and certain semiconductor materials: from raw materials to the knowledge of how to process them, and now to AI itself.

The likely winners and losers are uneven. Chinese AI firms and startups would be harder for Western entities to acquire, which, according to the July reporting, was a goal of the consultations. The exposed users are those who were never the target: European developers and smaller firms that rely on freely downloadable Chinese models could find access narrower if the controls reach them.

The timing is notable. The draft arrives while the two countries are making progress on AI safety dialogues and an incident-notification mechanism. That suggests competition over access and cooperation on safety can run side by side. Because the draft gives no effective date, and officials earlier considered applying curbs only to future models, Beijing keeps room to adjust how strictly it applies them, which could make the list a bargaining chip as well as a security measure.
