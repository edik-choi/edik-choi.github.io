Dockworkers and seafarers stopped work at ports across Europe and beyond on Friday, 30 October, in a coordinated protest against arms shipments through commercial ports and the risks faced by crews in war zones. More than 25 unions backed action across <mark>113 ports in 22 countries</mark>, with disruption reported at major shipping hubs and Greek ferry routes.

## What happened

### A one-day action spread across several regions

The walkout covered ports in Europe, North Africa and South Asia. Reuters named Hamburg, Valencia, Le Havre, Algeciras, Piraeus and Genoa, as well as Tangier Med in Morocco and Nhava Sheva in India. The campaign began with unions in Italy, France, Greece, Spain's Basque Country, Turkey and Morocco, then drew support from more than 25 labour organisations.

![Key figures from the international dockworkers' action: 113 ports, 22 countries and more than 25 unions](/images/2026-10-30-dockworkers-strike-113-ports-arms-shipments/port-network.svg)

Participation varied by port. Some workers stopped for a full day; others joined shorter walkouts or demonstrations. In Greece, a separate seafarers' stoppage over pay, safety and working conditions kept ships docked at several ports and disrupted ferry services. At Piraeus, the country's largest port, <mark>about 3,000 dockworkers and seafarers</mark> joined the walkout. Cargo operations were disrupted and ferries to Greek islands were suspended, Reuters reported. AP also documented demonstrations in Turkey and Italy and action at major French ports.

![Examples of shipping hubs named in reports of the strike, grouped by region](/images/2026-10-30-dockworkers-strike-113-ports-arms-shipments/global-hubs.svg)

The unions opposed using port facilities to move weapons, ammunition, military equipment and troops to conflict zones. Their call demanded an end to wars in the Middle East, including the war in Gaza. It also included workplace demands: higher pay, better conditions and the right to have doctors aboard vessels crossing dangerous waters. The action was therefore both an anti-war protest and a labour campaign led by people who work on ships and in ports.

### What was disrupted — and what remains uncertain

Before the strike, organisers said it could affect more than 40% of Mediterranean cargo and put over $2 billion in freight at risk. These were <mark>organisers' forecasts, not measured results</mark>. After the action, a campaign group gave Reuters an estimate involving 52,000 containers and $2.8 billion in cargo, but Reuters said it could not independently verify the impact. AP reported that the scale varied by port and that logistics experts expected a one-day stoppage to have a limited effect on global trade, with no widespread shortages expected.

## Background

### From a declaration to coordinated action

The strike followed a longer organising effort. In May 2026, unions from <mark>35 European countries</mark> published the Istanbul Declaration, calling for port facilities not to be used to transport weapons destined for war zones, Reuters reported. The October action extended that demand through a coordinated stoppage involving unions beyond the original group of countries.

Ports connect ships, warehouses and inland transport. The same commercial infrastructure can handle food, fuel, consumer goods, industrial components and military cargo. Workers do not control national arms-export licences, but they take part in loading, unloading and moving goods. A refusal to work can therefore create pressure where trade and military logistics meet. It can also affect civilian cargo handled at the same terminals.

### The safety concerns behind the protest

The campaign was also shaped by risks to seafarers. Greece's seafarers' union PENEN told Reuters that casualties among crews in conflict zones numbered in the hundreds, including people killed and severely injured. The union cited <mark>28 attacks on vessels in the Gulf</mark> during the first 21 days of October. It also said attacks in the Black Sea this year had left <mark>40 seafarers dead and hundreds wounded</mark>. These figures were claims made by the union, not independently verified totals in the report. Representatives of India's Water Transport Workers Federation separately told Reuters that about 15 Indian seafarers had died as a result of the Iran war, with others stranded at sea.

## Why it matters

### A local stoppage can expose a wider supply-chain weakness

The immediate economic effect appears more limited than the largest pre-strike forecasts suggested. But a short stoppage can add to congestion at busy ports. AP cited logistics experts who said a 24-hour action was unlikely to significantly disrupt global trade, while warning that perishable and time-sensitive shipments could still be affected. The same report described supply chains already under pressure from geopolitical tensions, climate events, restrictions on the Rhine and Panama Canal, and changing tariffs.

When ships, terminals and inland transport are already operating on tight schedules, a local interruption can make the next delay harder to absorb. The result may be queues, rescheduled sailings or extra handling costs rather than immediate shortages in shops.

### The protest brings workers into a debate over military logistics

The strike raises a question that governments and port operators cannot answer only with trade statistics: what responsibility do workers believe they have when commercial facilities are used to move military supplies? The stoppage does not create an arms embargo or decide whether a shipment is lawful. It does show that the people moving cargo can become part of the debate over where weapons go and what risks crews are asked to accept.

## What to watch

- **Port backlogs:** whether vessels face longer waits after the walkout, especially at Piraeus, Genoa and French ports.
- **Passenger services:** when normal ferry schedules to Greek islands resume.
- **Union coordination:** whether participating unions repeat the action or widen the list of ports.
- **Safety measures:** whether shipping companies or governments respond to demands for better protection and medical support for crews.
- **Verified trade effects:** actual port and shipping data will show whether the action caused more than temporary local disruption.

## Editor's Insight

The most consequential feature may be the network, not the amount of cargo delayed. A coordinated call across 113 ports gives workers in different countries a shared point of leverage, but participation varied: a full stoppage in one port could coincide with a short demonstration in another. The headline count measures reach, not proof that every port stopped operating.

The action also sits alongside the maritime insecurity described in our earlier report on [attacks on commercial shipping around the Strait of Hormuz](/blog/2026-10-23-iran-warns-us-bases-as-hormuz-tanker-attacks-hit-october-high/). The two stories show different vulnerabilities in the same system. Attacks threaten ships at sea; a labour stoppage interrupts activity where cargo enters or leaves the network. A one-day strike is not a blockade, but both episodes show how geopolitical conflict can travel through ordinary shipping routes.

Unions and anti-war campaigners gain visibility by making port logistics part of a public debate about military supply chains and seafarer safety. Port operators, shipping companies and importers may bear the cost if queues build, while governments and arms exporters face added scrutiny. Workers may also lose pay or face pressure from employers, so coordination does not automatically create lasting influence. The campaign's longer-term test will be whether it produces clearer crew protections, more transparent handling of military cargo or durable cross-border cooperation — outcomes that can be checked through policy changes and shipping data, not just organisers' projections.
