Storm Adam, a "DANA" weather system, flooded eastern Spain between 17 and 20 October, <mark>killing at least five people</mark> according to Euronews, with a regional tally counting four in the wider episode. Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez announced on 20 October that the affected area will be declared a disaster zone, as the storm revived memories of the deadly Valencia floods of 2024.

![Key-figure card: 632 mm of rain in 36 hours at Pego, Alicante, during Storm Adam](/images/2026-10-24-storm-adam-floods-eastern-spain-disaster-zone/key-card.svg)

## What happened

### How did Storm Adam flood Valencia and Alicante?

The system brought torrential rain from 17 to 19 October, and the regional emergency centre handled 1,876 incidents between the afternoon of 17 October and the morning of 20 October, according to The Watchers: 1,259 in Valencia province, 595 in Alicante and 22 in Castellón. Spain's weather agency AEMET called it the first high-impact DANA of the 2026–27 season.

The rainfall was exceptional in the La Safor and Marina Alta districts. AEMET provisional figures show <mark>632 mm in 36 hours at Pego</mark>, 430 of them in six hours, and 713 litres per square metre at L'Atzúvia in Alicante, Euronews reported. In Oliva, one of the worst-hit towns, <mark>600 litres per square metre fell in 24 hours</mark>. The rivers Girona and Vernisa and the Beniopa ravine overflowed.

![Bar chart of rainfall in Spain's most intense downpours: Oliva 1987 817, Turís 2024 772, L'Atzúvia 2026 713, Pego 2026 632, Oliva 2026 600 litres per square metre](/images/2026-10-24-storm-adam-floods-eastern-spain-disaster-zone/rainfall.svg)

Euronews ranks the episode among the most intense in Spain's records, behind Oliva in 1987 (817 litres in 24 hours) and the 2024 DANA at Turís (772 litres).

### How many people died and were rescued?

Euronews put the toll at <mark>at least five dead</mark>. The Watchers lists four deaths in the wider episode: a 79-year-old woman with reduced mobility found dead at home in Oliva; a 64-year-old man who suffered a heart attack at a flooded campsite in Oliva; a 54-year-old woman found dead after entering the sea at Sagunto; and a 35-year-old woman swept into a stream at Cabrera de Mar in Catalonia on 17 October. A farmer found dead in the Pego–Oliva marsh on 20 October was not counted, because preliminary information pointed to a medical emergency, and a death in Málaga province was not confirmed as storm-related. Because counts differ between outlets, the figure may change.

The Civil Guard reported 496 rescues and 2,141 evacuations in the Valencia region, while the regional emergency minister, Juan Carlos Valderrama, said all services together carried out <mark>more than 1,000 air and ground rescues</mark> on 19 October. About 600 people received help at Oliva's municipal centre, and 400 spent the night there.

### What disruption did it cause beyond Valencia?

On the Balearic Islands, Ibiza recorded more than 130 incidents and some airport operations were suspended because of standing water. Classes were suspended on Mallorca, Ibiza and Formentera, affecting around 160,000 students, and the military emergency unit was deployed to the islands. The heaviest rain then moved to Barcelona, Girona and Tarragona. In Valencia and Alicante, the AP-7 motorway stayed closed near Pilar de la Horadada and ten roads were affected.

## Background

### What is a DANA and why is eastern Spain exposed?

DANA stands for "Depresión Aislada en Niveles Altos", a cut-off low: a pocket of cold air high in the atmosphere that can bring heavy rain and storms. According to Euronews, the worst ones usually occur in September and October, mostly in southern and eastern Spain, where the Atlantic and the Mediterranean meet and mountains help form storm clouds. AEMET says the older term "gota fría" has been superseded by DANA.

### How does 2024 shape the response?

A DANA caused the October 2024 Valencia floods, which killed about 200 people, in what Euronews calls one of Europe's worst flood disasters since the late 1960s. Authorities were criticised then over emergency communications. This time the regional government used the ES-Alert mobile system, first over the weekend and again on Monday, urging people to avoid travel and stay away from waterways. AEMET had also issued a special notice on 18 October forecasting 200–300 mm in 12 hours along the southern Valencia coast, and the regional government <mark>locked down 145 municipalities</mark> on 19 October, lifting restrictions gradually the same afternoon.

### Is climate change making these storms worse?

Euronews notes that World Weather Attribution's rapid analysis of the 2024 event estimated that human-caused warming made the rainfall about 12% heavier and the event about twice as likely. Dr Friederike Otto said at the time: "No doubt about it, these explosive downpours were intensified by climate change." The team stressed that the analysis was partial. No equivalent study of Storm Adam had been reported at the time of writing.

## Why it matters

The storm tests whether the lessons of 2024 are working. Early warnings, preventive lockdowns and a large rescue deployment were in place this time, and the death toll, while painful, is far below that of 2024. Sánchez said DANAs in the Mediterranean "have always been frequent at this time of year, but not with this danger and intensity", and called for a "great national agreement" to improve the response to climate emergencies, with prevention at the centre of public policy. He cited torrential rains and the summer wildfires as evidence that institutions must be better prepared, according to ESdiario.

The disaster-zone declaration matters in practical terms. The Valencian government asked Madrid to designate the area as seriously affected by a civil-protection emergency and announced its own aid package, The Watchers reported. Sánchez said the national government would approve the declaration. Whether and how it is formally approved will determine how quickly affected households and towns receive support.

The event also fits a wider pattern. The UN Environment Programme said this week that holding warming to 1.5°C without overshoot [is no longer possible](/blog/2026-10-23-un-says-world-will-overshoot-1-5c-limit-emissions-gap-report/), and extreme rainfall is one of the impacts countries are being asked to adapt to.

## What to watch

- **Formal declaration:** whether the Spanish government approves the disaster-zone status and what aid, tax relief or compensation follows.
- **Death toll:** whether outlets and authorities converge on a single figure, including the status of cases excluded so far.
- **More DANA episodes:** AEMET called Adam the first high-impact DANA of the season; the risk period runs through autumn, and warnings remained in place in nine provinces on 20 October.
- **Attribution research:** any scientific analysis of how much climate change influenced the rainfall.
- **National pact:** whether Sánchez's call for a national agreement on climate emergencies draws support from regional governments, town councils, farmers and scientists.

## Editor's Insight

Storm Adam is a useful comparison case for 2024. The rainfall totals reported by AEMET are among the highest on record in Spain, yet the reported deaths are in single digits rather than around 200. That gap suggests, though one event cannot prove, that warnings and preventive lockdowns can change outcomes even when the hazard is comparable. The differences in rainfall location and timing also matter, so the comparison should not be pushed too far.

Politically, the response shows how a past disaster reshapes behaviour. Regional authorities that faced criticism in 2024 acted earlier and communicated more directly, and the central government moved quickly to announce disaster-zone status. A possible next test is the "great national agreement" Sánchez proposed: climate adaptation involves regions, municipalities and farmers, and agreeing on shared responsibilities could prove harder than agreeing on the need.

For the wider economy, the immediate costs appear concentrated in housing, transport and agriculture in a few districts rather than in national output. Still, repeated events could raise insurance and reconstruction costs for Mediterranean regions. That is a forecast, not an established fact, and would depend on how often such storms occur and on how much is spent on prevention.
