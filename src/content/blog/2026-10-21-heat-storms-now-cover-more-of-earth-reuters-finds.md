Heat waves are increasingly behaving less like isolated spells of hot weather and more like sprawling events that can persist for months and overlap across continents. A Reuters investigation published on October 20 calls the largest of these events “heat storms” and finds that they have become dramatically more frequent since the 1960s. The term is Reuters’ own analytical category, not a universally adopted meteorological classification, but the underlying question is urgent: how should societies prepare when extreme heat is widespread, persistent and connected to climate change?

## What happened

### How Reuters defines a heat storm

Reuters analysed hourly temperature data from more than one million grid squares covering the globe, reaching back to 1960, and consulted more than two dozen meteorologists and climate researchers. In its method, a heat wave consists of at least five consecutive days of unusually high temperatures. When such an event covers at least <mark>1% of Earth’s surface at its peak</mark>—an area Reuters compares with roughly half the size of Canada—it qualifies as a heat storm.

The analysis found an average of about <mark>five heat storms a year in the 1960s</mark>, compared with <mark>102 a year in the current decade</mark>. These are counts under Reuters’ definition, rather than figures from an international monitoring standard. The distinction matters: the category is new, but the temperature records it draws on are not.

### The record event lasted more than a year

The largest event identified by the investigation, named Heat Storm Vera, lasted <mark>367 days, from July 29, 2023, to July 29, 2024</mark>. At its widest, it affected nearly <mark>85% of the planet</mark>. Reuters traced its effects across six continents, including heat-related health emergencies, crop losses and damage to wildlife. The report links the event to a range of local experiences rather than suggesting that every place endured identical conditions.

![Key figure: Heat Storm Vera lasted 367 days and reached nearly 85% of Earth's surface](/images/2026-10-21-heat-storms-now-cover-more-of-earth-reuters-finds/vera.svg)

Reuters also describes a broader “cascade”: heat storms occurring in succession or at the same time. Its analysis says a cascade has continued without a break since July 6, 2008. Another named event, Heat Storm Raimundo, began in February 2026 and had reached almost three-quarters of Earth’s surface in the data described by Reuters. These labels are part of the project’s tracking system; they should not be confused with the official naming conventions used for tropical cyclones.

## Background

### Why are these events becoming more widespread?

Reuters found that atmospheric carbon dioxide levels rose by more than 30% from the 1960s to this decade. Over the same period, the average share of the planet likely to be covered by heat waves and heat storms rose more than twelvefold in the analysis. The report also identifies El Niño, a naturally occurring pattern of unusually warm Pacific waters that affects global weather, as an amplifier of some heat events.

That does not mean every heat wave can be attributed to one cause or that every region experiences the same trend at the same time. Weather still varies from place to place. The larger point is that a warming baseline makes extreme heat more likely and can help prolonged hot conditions spread across wider areas.

The United Nations Environment Programme’s 2026 Emissions Gap Report supplies the policy context. UNEP says the world has lost the possibility of limiting warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot, but argues that cutting emissions can still limit how high temperatures peak and help return warming below that level later this century. The Guardian’s October 21 report on the assessment likewise notes the gap between governments’ long-term pledges and the actions currently being taken.

## Why it matters

### Heat is a health emergency, not just uncomfortable weather

Extreme heat places pressure on the body, especially for older people, outdoor workers, pregnant people, children and those without reliable access to cooling. Reuters estimates that heat kills almost half a million people annually, while its case studies show how impacts can appear in places that rarely make the same global headline: students fainting in Philippine classrooms, football players needing hospital care in Texas, and communities facing dangerous outdoor work.

The scale of a heat storm can make these local risks harder to manage. When hot conditions persist, night-time relief may be limited, demand for electricity rises and hospitals or emergency services can face simultaneous pressure. Naming an event does not prevent harm, but it may help public agencies compare experiences, communicate risk and coordinate preparation across borders.

### Food, ecosystems and infrastructure are connected

The investigation describes crop damage in Ivory Coast, fires intensified by heat, and mass deaths among Amazon river dolphins during extreme temperatures. Heat can compound drought, increase water demand, stress livestock and crops, and strain electricity systems as more people rely on cooling. These impacts can travel through markets: poor harvests can raise prices, while damaged infrastructure can make it harder to move food and provide services.

The effects are uneven. A region that benefits from a longer growing season may still face water shortages or dangerous heat for workers. The same event can create winners and losers across agriculture, energy and public health. That is why broad global averages should be paired with local exposure and vulnerability data.

## What to watch

- **The Heat Storm Tracker:** whether Reuters’ new tracking tool makes it easier for researchers, journalists and public agencies to compare events using a consistent method.
- **Early-warning systems:** whether forecasts lead to practical measures such as adjusted school and work hours, cooling centres, public alerts and protection for outdoor workers.
- **Energy and water planning:** whether utilities and cities prepare for sustained demand rather than only short heat spikes.
- **Climate commitments:** whether governments turn pledges into near-term emissions cuts. UNEP says current plans are insufficient, while still identifying policies that could be expanded.
- **Measurement and scrutiny:** whether independent researchers reproduce or challenge Reuters’ proposed category and findings. “Heat storm” is useful as a communication tool only if its definitions and data remain transparent.

## Editor's Insight

The most consequential part of the Reuters investigation may be less the new name than the change in scale it makes visible. People understand a heat wave in their town; they may not immediately connect it with a school on another continent, a damaged crop elsewhere or stressed wildlife in a river basin. A shared map and a consistent definition can make those separate experiences look like parts of a wider pattern. That is a reporting framework, not proof that every local impact has a single cause.

There is also a risk in making a new label sound like an official scientific category. Reuters developed “heat storm” for this analysis, and the distinction should stay clear in public communication. Its value will depend on whether the underlying method is open to scrutiny, whether other researchers find it useful, and whether the label helps decision-makers act rather than simply creating another headline.

The policy lesson is more straightforward. Better names and trackers can improve awareness, but they cannot substitute for reducing greenhouse gas emissions or preparing communities for heat that is already occurring. UNEP’s assessment makes the same distinction between the temperature goal that is now out of reach without overshoot and the still-important task of limiting that overshoot. Governments can pursue both adaptation and emissions cuts: protect people from the next hot spell while reducing the conditions that make future events more dangerous.
