Brazil's presidential runoff on Sunday, 25 October, produced a count that swung back and forth through the night. Flávio Bolsonaro led early, then President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva overtook him, and <mark>Lula led with 50.92% at 70% counted</mark>, according to figures reported by France 24. The Superior Electoral Court (TSE) had not declared a winner in the sources reviewed for this post.

## What happened

### How the Brazil runoff count unfolded

Votes from abroad were counted first, and Lula had won there in the first round. France 24 listed the TSE figures through the night: Lula at 51.16% with 5% counted, Bolsonaro at 50.04% with 13% counted, Bolsonaro at 50.41% with 20% counted, Lula at 50.14% with 50% counted and <mark>Lula at 50.92% with 70% counted</mark>. The margin stayed under one point for much of the count.

![Line chart of Lula's share of the vote as the count advanced: 51.16% at 5% counted, 49.59% at 20%, 50.14% at 50% and 50.92% at 70%](/images/2026-10-26-lula-pulls-ahead-in-brazil-runoff-count-against-flavio-bolsonaro/count-chart.svg)

Reuters, quoted by The Jerusalem Post, reported that Lula overtook Bolsonaro once about half the vote was counted, as ballots from his northeastern strongholds arrived. Lula's allies were already claiming victory on social media. AtlasIntel chief executive Andrei Roman told Reuters that Lula would be re-elected, and France 24 reported that its headline count had Lula "on the verge of victory" with more than 80% of votes counted.

### Why the voting was extended in some areas

Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin, formerly Lula's lawyer, granted a Lula campaign request to <mark>extend voting by two hours</mark> after transport problems in three cities in Bahia, Reuters reported. Lula asked for extensions in at least nine states, and Flávio Bolsonaro asked for them in at least three, citing bad weather. A Bahia court ordered the arrest of Salvador's mobility secretary after at least 75 buses were kept in garages on election day. The TSE paused its count for about two hours, according to Reuters.

Lula said "something abominable is happening" in the election. Flávio's brother Eduardo Bolsonaro said the ruling "doesn't inspire credibility." Constitutional lawyer Vera Chemim told Reuters the losing side might contest the result.

## Background

### Why this runoff was so close

On 4 October, <mark>Flávio Bolsonaro won the first round with 47.03%</mark> (56,104,503 votes) against Lula's 45.16% (53,879,538), according to the Rio Times, which cites the TSE. Neither passed the majority needed to win outright. More than 158 million Brazilians were registered to vote, and first-round abstention of 21.08% was the highest for a first round since 1998.

![Bar chart of the first-round result on 4 October: Flávio Bolsonaro 47.03%, Lula 45.16%](/images/2026-10-26-lula-pulls-ahead-in-brazil-runoff-count-against-flavio-bolsonaro/first-round.svg)

Lula, 80, is seeking a fourth term with Geraldo Alckmin as his running mate. Flávio, 45, of the Liberal Party, was endorsed by his father, former president Jair Bolsonaro, who is serving a sentence of 27 years and 3 months under house arrest after the Supreme Court convicted him over a plot to overturn the 2022 election. He denies the charges. Flávio has promised a pardon, which legal experts say would not restore his father's eligibility. Our earlier post on how [the runoff tightened](/blog/2026-10-16-brazil-runoff-tightens-between-lula-and-flavio-bolsonaro/) covers the campaign.

### What the two candidates proposed on the economy

Brazil is rated below investment grade (BB), and public debt is about 93% of GDP, according to the Rio Times. Lula backs keeping the fiscal framework, lasting interest-rate cuts and an income-tax exemption for salaries up to R$5,000 funded by taxing the very rich. Flávio proposed spending cuts, including at least ten fewer ministries, budget surpluses, privatisation and about R$900 billion of infrastructure investment over four years. Brazil also faces an extra 25% US tariff on most goods.

## Why it matters

### Why the Brazil election result matters beyond Brazil

Brazil is Latin America's largest country, and Reuters notes that Lula and his allies see the runoff as a chance to <mark>slow the region's conservative shift</mark>. Reuters also reported a campaign inside the US State Department to help the Bolsonaros regain power; President Donald Trump has not endorsed a candidate and said "we are watching."

The dispute over voting hours shows how fragile trust in the process is. Jair Bolsonaro refused to concede after losing in 2022, and his supporters later stormed government buildings, according to Reuters. A narrow result with legal challenges could test institutions again.

![Card showing 158 million registered voters in Brazil](/images/2026-10-26-lula-pulls-ahead-in-brazil-runoff-count-against-flavio-bolsonaro/voters-card.svg)

Markets matter too. Brazilian markets reopen on Monday, 26 October, and the central bank's next rate decision is on 4 November. The real traded near R$5.05 per dollar on 19 October, the Rio Times reports. <mark>Polls favoured Flávio and prediction markets favoured Lula</mark>, and the Rio Times says polls had missed the first round.

## What to watch

- **Official result:** whether the TSE declares a winner and what the final margin is.
- **Concession:** whether the losing candidate accepts the result or files legal challenges over the extended voting.
- **Congress:** parties backing Flávio won 259 of 513 Chamber seats, according to the Rio Times. Constitutional amendments need 308 deputies and 49 of 81 senators.
- **Key dates:** the central bank decides on rates on 4 November, the winner is sworn in on 5 January 2027 and the new Congress takes office on 1 February 2027.

## Editor's Insight

If the count holds, the outcome would be a reversal of the first round. The first-round lead of about two points did not carry over, which suggests the northeast's late-counted vote and turnout decided the race. That is an interpretation of the count order, not a confirmed explanation.

The larger question may be legitimacy rather than the margin. With both campaigns alleging obstacles to voting and a Supreme Court justice with a past link to one candidate deciding on extensions, a loser could challenge the result even if the numbers are clear. Past practice, such as the 2022 refusal to concede, makes that a possibility worth watching, not a prediction.

For markets, a Lula win would likely mean continuity in the fiscal framework that he says he supports, while a Flávio win would have meant a stronger push for spending cuts and privatisation. Congress, where Flávio's allies hold 259 of 513 seats, may limit either president's room to move. These are possibilities, not forecasts.

Ten days ago, two polls had Flávio Bolsonaro two points ahead, and our earlier report noted that about 5.3 million first-round votes for eliminated conservatives were left without an endorsement. The count suggests that bloc did not move to him in large numbers. If the result holds, Lula is the clear winner, while the Bolsonaro family keeps its hold on Congress.
