Jordanian security forces have arrested three armed suspects over the shooting at a funeral in a Greek Orthodox church in Karak governorate. According to Al Jazeera, <mark>eight people were killed and 40 wounded</mark> on Friday 23 October; Asharq Al-Awsat and The Globe and Mail put the toll at nine. <mark>No group has claimed responsibility</mark>, and officials have not said whether the suspects belonged to a militant organisation.

![Key-figure card: 40 people wounded in the Karak church attack, with 8 to 9 killed and 3 suspects arrested](/images/2026-10-26-jordan-arrests-three-suspects-after-karak-church-funeral-attack/key-card.svg)

## What happened

### What do we know about the Karak church attack?

Three gunmen opened fire on a funeral at a Greek Orthodox church in the village of Hamoud, in Karak governorate about 120 km (75 miles) southwest of the capital, Amman, according to Al Jazeera. Witnesses told reporters the shooting lasted several minutes, as reported by Asharq Al-Awsat.

The death toll is reported differently. Al Jazeera, citing Jordan's Ministry of Health, says eight people were killed and 40 injured. Asharq Al-Awsat and The Globe and Mail report nine dead and 40 wounded. The Globe and Mail noted that an earlier headline on its own page had said seven dead, which suggests the figure rose as reports were updated. This post gives both figures rather than choosing one.

As of Monday, <mark>27 of the injured remained in hospital</mark>, and one patient was in critical condition after surgery, the director of Prince Ali Military Hospital in Karak told Al Jazeera.

![Bar chart of the Karak church attack casualties: 40 wounded, 27 still in hospital, 1 critical](/images/2026-10-26-jordan-arrests-three-suspects-after-karak-church-funeral-attack/casualties.svg)

### How were the three suspects arrested?

Security forces found the three men early on Monday 26 October hiding in an abandoned house in the village of Al-Jadeeda in Karak, after days of searching. They refused to surrender and attacked the forces, who returned fire, Al Jazeera reported. A government source told the outlet that <mark>the fighting lasted about three hours</mark>.

All three suspects were wounded and arrested, and one was in critical condition. No security personnel were reported injured. Firearms and live ammunition were found at the site, according to the Petra News Agency, as cited by Al Jazeera.

### What have Jordanian officials said?

The Public Security Directorate said "Terrorism has no place among us" and that the suspects had met their "just fate". It did not name any affiliated group. Government Communication Minister Mohammad Momani praised the joint operation and confirmed that the three were injured and in custody.

King Abdullah II offered condolences to the families and said those responsible would face the "harshest penalties", and he ordered a review of security procedures for responding to attacks, according to Al Jazeera. Asharq Al-Awsat described his instruction to the security forces as an "iron fist" response. Interior Minister Mazen al-Faraya said <mark>authorities are examining what draws young people toward violence</mark> and that anyone found to have taken part would be held accountable.

## Background

Karak is home to many members of Jordan's Christian minority, which <mark>numbers about 300,000 of the country's 11.5 million people</mark>, according to Al Jazeera. That is roughly 2.6 percent of the population by those figures.

The Globe and Mail describes the attack as rare and aimed at the Christian minority, and calls such violence highly unusual in a country it describes as relatively stable. Authorities say they have foiled several extremist plots over the years, but the report does not describe specific earlier attacks on churches or Christians.

The attackers' motive has not been stated. Officials called them "extremist terrorists" on the day of the attack, but the interior minister said it was too early to know whether they had links to known groups inside or outside Jordan, as reported by The Globe and Mail.

## Why it matters

For Jordan, an attack on mourners inside a church is a test of an image of stability in a region where wars continue on several borders. Reports describe such violence as rare, and the scale, with dozens wounded, adds to the shock. The government's quick response, combined with the king's order to review security procedures, shows that officials see the episode as a failure that needs a systemic answer as well as arrests.

For Jordan's Christians, the immediate concern is safety at places of worship and at funerals. The Globe and Mail reported that armed police patrolled near mourners at the victims' funeral on Sunday.

For the wider region, the main question is attribution. Until the suspects are questioned and any claim of responsibility is verified, claims about links to militant networks remain unconfirmed.

## What to watch

- **Condition of the suspects:** One of the three was in critical condition, which could limit how much investigators can learn from them.
- **Any claim of responsibility:** No group has claimed the attack. A claim, or a statement on the suspects' affiliation, would shape the response.
- **The security review:** The king ordered a review of response procedures. Whether it leads to changes in how places of worship are protected is the next signal.
- **The final toll:** With 27 people still in hospital and one in critical condition, the figures could change, and the eight-versus-nine discrepancy may be resolved by an official statement.

## Editor's Insight

The speed of the arrests, within three days, suggests that Jordan's security services retained a strong capacity to track armed groups even after an attack that officials describe as unusual. The three-hour firefight, as described by a government source, also shows that the suspects were armed and prepared. That is a reminder that preventing an attack and solving one are different tasks.

The government's language is notable. By ordering a review of security procedures and by the interior minister's focus on what draws young people to violence, officials are framing the attack as both a policing problem and a social one. If that framing holds, it could mean policy attention on youth radicalisation, though that is a possibility rather than a stated programme.

Finally, the gap between eight and nine deaths is a small but real example of how early casualty counts shift. Readers should treat the figures as provisional until Jordan's health ministry publishes a final number.

The heaviest cost falls on Jordan's Christians, about 2.6% of the population, for whom funerals and churches now come with armed patrols. For the state, the loss is to an image of stability in a region with wars on several borders. Until the suspects' motives and any outside links are established, it remains unclear who, if anyone, the attackers sought to serve.
