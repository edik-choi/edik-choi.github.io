Nigeria has confirmed its first case of Ebola in the current outbreak, with <mark>the Bundibugyo strain</mark> that is driving the epidemic in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). The patient is a woman in the capital, Abuja, who had travelled from Uganda, and the World Health Organization (WHO) says Nigeria is <mark>the fifth country to detect the virus</mark> since the outbreak was declared in May.

![Key-figure card: Nigeria is the fifth country to detect Ebola Bundibugyo in the outbreak, with 44 contacts identified](/images/2026-10-22-nigeria-confirms-first-ebola-case-bundibugyo-strain/key-card.svg)

## What happened

### What do we know about Nigeria's first Ebola case?

According to the WHO, Nigeria confirmed one imported case in the Federal Capital Territory, with laboratory tests confirming the virus on 20 October. The patient is a woman with recent travel to Uganda. Al Jazeera reported that she arrived in Abuja on 3 October, and the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control and Prevention (NCDC) said she was tested on 19 October after officials learned of her travel. NCDC laboratory director Olajumoke Babatunde said three levels of molecular tests were run.

The WHO says her symptoms began on 7 October, four days after she arrived, and that she sought care at a private facility in Abuja on 11 October. She is in isolation and receiving treatment, Health Minister Muhammad Ali Pate told the broadcaster NTA, as reported by Al Jazeera.

![Timeline of the Abuja patient from arrival on 3 October to WHO confirmation on 20 October](/images/2026-10-22-nigeria-confirms-first-ebola-case-bundibugyo-strain/timeline.svg)

### How many contacts are being traced?

<mark>44 contacts</mark> have been identified and are being monitored, most of them healthcare workers, Al Jazeera reported. They include the patient's husband, a Swiss national who lives with her in Abuja. Officials are also tracing fellow passengers on the flight from Uganda, though they do not believe she showed symptoms during the trip. The WHO said infection prevention and control was stepped up at the private hospital where she sought care.

### Who is the patient?

An unnamed official told AFP that she is a diplomat whose condition has deteriorated to multi-organ failure, according to Al Jazeera and Dawn. Details of her identity and the source of her infection are still being investigated, the WHO said.

## Background

### Why is the Bundibugyo strain a concern?

There is <mark>no vaccine or specific treatment</mark> for the Bundibugyo strain, both Al Jazeera and Dawn reported. That leaves containment dependent on finding cases quickly, isolating them and following up their contacts. The WHO says rapid case identification and timely contact follow-up are "critical to contain the spread of the virus."

### How big is the outbreak in the DRC?

The outbreak was declared in the DRC on 15 May 2026. According to Dawn (AFP), it has killed <mark>4,381 people from more than 9,000 confirmed cases</mark>, making it the deadliest in the country's history. Kenya, which [reported its first local transmission earlier this month](/blog/2026-10-19-kenya-ebola-cases-rise-to-seven-health-workers-infected/), has now reported nine cases linked to an imported case, the WHO says.

![Bar chart of Ebola outbreak figures: more than 9,000 DRC cases, 4,381 DRC deaths, nine Kenyan cases and one Nigerian case](/images/2026-10-22-nigeria-confirms-first-ebola-case-bundibugyo-strain/outbreak-figures.svg)

### What happened in Nigeria in 2014?

Al Jazeera notes that Nigeria has faced Ebola before. In 2014 the first case, in Lagos, was controlled within three months, with 20 infections and eight deaths. That outbreak was part of the 2013–2016 West African epidemic, which killed more than 11,000 people.

## Why it matters

Nigeria is Africa's most populous country, and Abuja is a major air hub, so an imported case raises the stakes for regional containment. The spread to a fifth country shows the outbreak is no longer confined to the DRC and its neighbours. Africa CDC Director-General Jean Kaseya said the outbreak is "no longer one country's fight" and, as reported by Dawn, warned of "an extremely serious threat." He plans to convene African health ministers next week to strengthen the cross-border response and review hospital capacity, Al Jazeera reported.

The timing of the diagnosis is also a concern. Ayoade Alakija, a Nigerian physician, told Al Jazeera that <mark>12 days passed between her falling ill and her testing</mark>, potentially exposing others. The fact that most of the identified contacts are healthcare workers suggests exposure at the facility where she was first treated, though officials have not said how many of them may have been infected.

The WHO advises against any restriction of travel to, or trade with, affected countries, and recommends that countries in the region maintain surveillance, keep laboratories and response teams ready, reinforce infection control in public and private facilities and heighten monitoring at points of entry.

## What to watch

- **Contact monitoring:** Whether any of the 44 contacts, especially the health workers, develop symptoms during the monitoring period.
- **Source of infection:** Uganda's health authorities and the WHO are working to establish where the patient was exposed. Public statements so far leave this open.
- **Ministers' meeting:** Africa CDC's planned meeting of health ministers next week.
- **Case counts:** Totals in the DRC, Kenya and now Nigeria can change quickly. Check the WHO and national health agencies for updates.
- **Vaccine trials:** Candidate vaccines for the Bundibugyo strain are in early human trials, as noted in earlier reporting.

## Editor's Insight

The Nigerian case is a reminder that the weak point in an Ebola response is often the gap between a patient's first symptoms and the moment the disease is named. Both Kenya and Nigeria now have imported cases where the patient was ill for days before testing, a pattern the reporting itself points to. A plausible reading is that, without a vaccine, speed of recognition is the only lever health systems have, and that private clinics, where this patient first sought care, are a place where that recognition can lag.

There is also a contrast with 2014. Nigeria then contained an outbreak of 20 infections within three months. Whether that past experience helps this time depends on how many of the 44 contacts, and any others yet to be found, are traced and monitored. The sources do not yet say whether any contact is ill, so a quick containment is a possibility, not a finding.

For the wider region, the main question is travel and trade policy. The WHO's advice against restrictions sits against a political reality in which governments often act on fear of imported cases. If more countries detect cases, pressure for border measures may grow. That is analysis rather than a reported development, and it will depend on how the case count moves in the coming weeks.
