A Spanish humanitarian charity, Salvamento Marítimo Humanitario (SMH), announced on Wednesday, 14 October 2026, that it is <mark>postponing its next central Mediterranean rescue mission</mark> because of soaring fuel costs. According to Reuters, the delay runs until 2027, while Al Jazeera describes it as "until next year". The group said on X that <mark>fuel prices had doubled</mark>.

## What happened

SMH operates the Aita Mari, a former fishing vessel that was converted into a rescue ship in 2017. Arab News, carrying a Reuters report from Madrid, says the charity has suspended operations on the central Mediterranean route and will shift its activity to waters off Spain's Valencia coast and around the islands of Mallorca and Menorca.

The charity has also launched a crowdfunding campaign to cover the additional costs. The doubling of fuel prices is SMH's own figure; the reports do not give per-litre prices or say how many people the group had rescued.

The central Mediterranean route runs from North Africa to Italy and southern France. Reuters calls it <mark>one of the world's most dangerous migrant routes</mark>, used by people from Africa and the Middle East, often in overcrowded or unseaworthy boats. Many departures are from the Libyan coast, according to Al Jazeera.

![Bar chart comparing about 98,000 sea arrivals to Europe in 2025 with about 60,000 in 2026](/images/2026-10-15-spanish-rescue-charity-delays-mediterranean-mission-over-fuel-costs/arrivals.svg)

Crossings overall have fallen this year. Al Jazeera, citing figures from the International Organization for Migration (IOM), reports that about 60,000 migrants and refugees have reached Europe by boat so far in 2026, <mark>down 39%</mark> from roughly 98,000 in the same period of 2025. The same report says <mark>at least 2,292 people</mark> have died or gone missing attempting the crossing this year.

## Background

SMH is not the only charity feeling the pressure. Reuters notes that fellow Spanish group Open Arms has also scaled back its central Mediterranean missions in recent years, citing rising fuel costs and Italy's policy of assigning rescue ships to ports in northern Italy. That policy can <mark>require ships to sail two or three days</mark> away from their operating area, which raises costs and reduces the time available for search and rescue, according to Reuters and Al Jazeera.

Italian politics is part of the picture. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni's government has repeatedly argued that humanitarian rescue vessels encourage migrants to attempt the crossing, according to Reuters. Aid groups have contested that view, and the reports do not settle the question.

The fuel shock itself has a wider cause. According to Reuters, marine fuel prices spiked after the United States and Israel attacked Iran in late February, reached record highs in March, and have since softened but remained volatile. At an industry meeting in Singapore, the world's largest bunkering hub, Reuters reported that prices for very low-sulphur fuel oil were <mark>up more than 60% from pre-war levels</mark>. The same report said supply at major hubs has eased compared with March and April, although refuelling costs remain higher, and that bunkering in Fujairah, in the United Arab Emirates, was about 40% of its pre-war level.

The disruption around the Strait of Hormuz has also reached other sectors. Our earlier report on the [stalled UN plan to move fertiliser through the strait](/blog/2026-10-15-hormuz-fertiliser-plan-stalls-un-warns-of-food-crisis/) describes how the same conflict is affecting food supply chains.

## Why it matters

Rescue charities operate on narrow budgets, and fuel is one of their largest running costs. For a small ship that must travel far from its home port to reach the central Mediterranean, a doubling of fuel prices changes the arithmetic of whether a mission is viable at all. SMH's decision shows how a conflict thousands of kilometres away can reach humanitarian work at sea.

The timing is notable because deaths have continued even as arrivals fell. Al Jazeera, citing the IOM, puts the toll at <mark>at least 2,292 dead or missing</mark> this year. Fewer crossings do not necessarily mean a safer route, and fewer dedicated rescue ships mean fewer vessels in position to respond to boats in distress. Reuters notes that the central Mediterranean is among the most dangerous migration routes in the world.

![Key figure card showing at least 2,292 people dead or missing on Europe's sea routes in 2026](/images/2026-10-15-spanish-rescue-charity-delays-mediterranean-mission-over-fuel-costs/deaths.svg)

The decision also feeds a political argument. Governments that favour tighter controls, such as Italy's, say rescue ships act as a pull factor. Charities say that their presence is a response to deaths at sea, not their cause. A fuel-driven withdrawal of ships will be read differently by each side, but the practical effect is a reduction in rescue capacity on a route where, in the words of the reports, crossings are often made in overcrowded or unseaworthy boats.

## What to watch

- **Fundraising:** whether SMH's crowdfunding campaign covers the extra costs and whether the mission can resume in 2027 as planned.
- **Other charities:** whether Open Arms or other groups further reduce or suspend central Mediterranean operations.
- **Fuel prices:** whether marine fuel prices keep easing from their March records, and how developments around the Strait of Hormuz affect them.
- **Casualty figures:** updated IOM data on arrivals and on deaths and disappearances on the central Mediterranean route.
- **Italian policy:** any change to the practice of assigning rescue ships to distant northern ports, which charities say raises their costs.
