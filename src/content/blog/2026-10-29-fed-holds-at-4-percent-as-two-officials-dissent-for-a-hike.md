The US Federal Reserve kept its benchmark rate unchanged on 28 October, <mark>holding the federal funds target range at 3¾ to 4 percent</mark>. The decision was widely expected, but the vote was not unanimous: <mark>two officials, Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, wanted a quarter-point increase</mark>. With the next meeting six weeks away, the question for borrowers and markets is whether a further US interest rate hike in December is coming.

## What happened

The Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) released its statement at 2:00 p.m. Eastern time. It described economic activity as expanding at a strong pace and said job gains have kept pace with the workforce, with the unemployment rate little changed. On prices it was blunt: <mark>"inflation remains elevated"</mark>, and the committee said more information would help it set a policy stance that returns inflation to its 2 percent goal in a timely way.

![Key-figure card showing the Fed's unchanged 3.75–4% target range, a 10–2 vote and about 85% market odds of a December hike](/images/2026-10-29-fed-holds-at-4-percent-as-two-officials-dissent-for-a-hike/key-card.svg)

### How the 10–2 Fed vote split

Ten members voted to hold. Cook, a governor, and Hammack, president of the Cleveland Fed, voted for a quarter-point rise. A split of this kind shows that a part of the committee already sees the current level as too low, even though a majority chose to wait.

![Dot diagram of the 12 voting members: 10 for holding rates, 2 for a quarter-point hike](/images/2026-10-29-fed-holds-at-4-percent-as-two-officials-dissent-for-a-hike/vote-split.svg)

### What Warsh said about politics and the midterms

Chair Kevin Warsh told his press conference that <mark>politics "played no role"</mark> in the decision, according to Yahoo Finance's live coverage. The US midterm elections are about six days away. He said financial conditions have tightened but declined to call them "tight", and that the policy so far is beginning to work, though there are no firm conclusions. He also pushed back on the view that rate hikes will not affect AI investment, saying interest rates "affect directly or indirectly almost everything".

Warsh announced a change for 2027: one of the eight scheduled meetings will focus on strategy, with no rate vote or policy statement.

### How markets reacted to the Fed decision

Stocks whipsawed, dipping after the statement, rallying during the press conference and then giving much of that back. Yahoo Finance reported that the <mark>10-year Treasury yield rose above 5%</mark>, the 30-year yield was near 5.33%, and mortgage rates were around 7.4%. Fed funds futures put the chance of a quarter-point hike at the next meeting, on 9 December, at about 85%.

## Background

### Why the Fed raised rates in September

The hold follows a hike. On 16 September the committee raised the target range by a quarter point to 3¾–4 percent, by a 12–0 vote. That statement also said inflation "remains elevated" and that the move was meant to help inflation return to 2 percent sooner.

### Why inflation is the central issue

The latest inflation data explains the caution. The September consumer price index, released on 14 October, showed <mark>headline inflation of 3.7% year over year</mark>, against 3.6% expected and 3.4% in August. Core inflation, which excludes food and energy, was 2.6%, against 2.5% expected and 2.4% in August. Prices rose 0.6% on the month.

![Bar chart of US CPI inflation: headline 3.4% in August and 3.7% in September, core 2.4% in August and 2.6% in September](/images/2026-10-29-fed-holds-at-4-percent-as-two-officials-dissent-for-a-hike/cpi-bars.svg)

Energy has been a recurring pressure. As this site reported when [tanker attacks near Hormuz reached an October high](/blog/2026-10-23-iran-warns-us-bases-as-hormuz-tanker-attacks-hit-october-high/), the Iran war has kept fuel supplies tight, and the same strain showed up when [Europe entered winter with record-low gas storage](/blog/2026-10-24-eu-enters-winter-with-record-low-gas-storage/).

## Why it matters

The US policy rate sets the price of dollar borrowing everywhere. A 10-year Treasury yield above 5% feeds into mortgage costs, corporate loans and the debt burdens of governments and companies that borrow in dollars. A mortgage rate around 7.4% weighs on US housing. For other central banks, a Fed that is leaning toward further hikes limits how far they can cut without weakening their currencies.

![Timeline: Fed hike on 16 September, CPI release on 14 October, Fed hold on 28 October, next meeting 9 December](/images/2026-10-29-fed-holds-at-4-percent-as-two-officials-dissent-for-a-hike/timeline.svg)

The decision also lands days before the midterms, which is why Warsh addressed politics directly. A central bank that is seen to move for electoral reasons risks its credibility, and a hold that was explained by data, with two dissents in the hawkish direction, gives the committee a record that it did not ease under pressure.

## What to watch

- **9 December meeting:** futures pricing implies a quarter-point hike is the base case. A softer inflation reading before then would test that.
- **Next CPI release:** whether headline inflation stays at or above 3.7% and whether core keeps edging up from 2.6%.
- **Dissent count:** if more members join Cook and Hammack, a hike becomes harder to avoid.
- **Long-term yields:** whether the 10-year stays above 5% and what that does to mortgage rates and equities.
- **The strategy-only meeting in 2027:** how the Fed uses a meeting with no rate vote to explain its framework.

## Editor's Insight

The two dissents matter more than the unchanged rate. When the committee raised rates on 16 September it was unanimous; six weeks later it is split, and the split runs toward tightening. That is the opposite of a committee preparing to ease. Combined with a statement that kept the word "elevated" for inflation and an economy described as strong, the signal is that the debate has moved from whether to hike again to when.

Warsh's insistence that politics played no role is worth reading as a response to a specific risk. A chair appointed in a politically charged environment, facing midterms within a week, has an incentive to look independent, and a hold with hawkish dissents does that more convincingly than a cut would. It remains a possibility, not a fact, that the same logic leads to a December hike. The nearly 85% market pricing is a forecast built on fed funds futures, and it can move quickly if inflation data change.

The winners and losers are fairly clear. Savers and holders of short-term Treasuries earn more, and the dollar tends to be supported. Home buyers facing mortgage rates near 7.4%, indebted governments, and emerging-market borrowers with dollar debt carry the cost. The energy link also deserves attention: if inflation is being pushed by oil and gas prices tied to the Iran war, as this site's earlier coverage of Hormuz suggests, then the Fed is raising rates against a supply shock that rate hikes cannot fix, which is the hardest kind of inflation for a central bank to handle.

Finally, the warning about AI investment is a reminder that the technology boom is exposed to the same rate environment as everything else. If long yields stay above 5%, the cost of financing data centres rises too, a point that investors in the sector are likely to test over the winter.
