Yemen's Iran-backed Houthi movement has launched a wave of missile and drone strikes on Saudi Arabia, and <mark>at least 19 people have been killed</mark>, according to Saudi authorities. On 18 October Egypt's National Defense Council pledged "all necessary and possible forms of support" to defend the kingdom, as Riyadh seeks help from allies.

## What happened

### The airport strikes in Riyadh

The Houthis have repeatedly hit Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport, causing waves of flight cancellations, AP reported on 17 October. CBS News reported that a strike on 10 October <mark>killed 13 people, including a U.S. citizen, and wounded more than 300</mark>. Another strike on 11 October killed one more person. The airport was suspended through 17 October, and Saudi authorities are investigating why air defences did not intercept the attacks.

![Key figures: at least 19 killed and more than 300 wounded in Houthi strikes on Saudi airports](/images/2026-10-19-houthi-attacks-on-saudi-airports-draw-egypt-pledge/toll.svg)

Counts differ slightly by outlet: AP and UPI give at least 19 dead, citing Saudi authorities, while CBS News reports at least 20 deaths over 6–12 October. Abha International Airport was also attacked, per CCTV footage released on 15 October, and the rebels hit the headquarters of the national oil company, Aramco, AP reported.

### Saudi-led strikes and the Egyptian pledge

On 13 October the Saudi-led coalition confirmed strikes on Houthi oil sites in western Yemen, including Sanaa, Dhamar and Saada, calling them a response to the deadliest attack on the kingdom in years, CBS News reported. The same day, two Houthi ballistic missiles aimed at eastern Saudi Arabia were intercepted; debris falling on Riyadh injured four people and damaged 15 homes, and a limited fire broke out at an Aramco administrative building with no injuries reported.

UPI reported that Egypt's National Defense Council, chaired by President Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, vowed support but gave no detail. Egyptian lawmakers are expected to discuss authorising a troop deployment, according to state media, and el-Sisi said any Egyptian action would be defensive. Neither country has publicly confirmed a Saudi request for military help.

## Background

### Why the Houthis target Saudi Arabia

The Houthis are a Shiite movement rooted in northern Yemen. According to AP, they attack Saudi Arabia because it supports Yemen's internationally recognised government. Saudi Arabia joined the war in 2015 after the Houthis seized much of northern Yemen, including Sanaa, and carried out airstrikes and a naval blockade for seven years before a 2022 ceasefire. In July 2026 the Houthis <mark>resumed attacks and declared a blockade on Saudi shipping</mark>.

### How the Houthis grew stronger

AP reports the Houthis recruited <mark>up to 350,000 fighters by 2024</mark>, up from about 30,000 in 2015, and can make much of their weaponry domestically at lower cost than Saudi interceptors. U.N. and other experts say Iran and its allies in Lebanon and Iraq provide expertise, training and weapons; Iran denies arming the Houthis, which would violate a U.N. embargo.

![Bar chart: Houthi fighters rose from about 30,000 in 2015 to up to 350,000 in 2024](/images/2026-10-19-houthi-attacks-on-saudi-airports-draw-egypt-pledge/fighters.svg)

The escalation sits inside a wider regional war. CBS News reports that the Iran war began in late February after U.S. and Israeli strikes on Tehran, that Iran continues to disrupt the Strait of Hormuz, and that the U.S. has kept a naval blockade of Iranian ports since 14 July. Earlier coverage here looked at how the [Hormuz fertiliser plan stalled](/blog/2026-10-15-hormuz-fertiliser-plan-stalls-un-warns-of-food-crisis/).

## Why it matters

The strikes threaten Saudi plans to become a global hub for business, tourism and sports, AP noted, and school classes were cancelled. Delta has postponed planned Atlanta–Riyadh flights until March 2027, and the U.S. State Department now requires government employees to get special authorisation to use any Saudi airport. <mark>Brent crude traded above $105 a barrel on 12 October</mark>, a two-week high, according to CBS News.

For Yemen, the escalation lands on a population already strained: suspected cholera cases there tripled in three weeks in September, per the WHO, as covered in our [report on Yemen's cholera outbreak](/blog/2026-10-14-yemen-cholera-cases-triple-as-fighting-displaces-families/).

## What to watch

- **Egypt's parliament:** whether lawmakers authorise a troop deployment.
- **U.S. role:** UPI reports Washington rejected Saudi requests last month to strike Houthi positions but provides intelligence; Trump said on 11 October the U.S. "may" join.
- **Houthi threats:** a spokesman threatened Aramco facilities and Riyadh's airport if Sanaa is struck again.
- **Interceptor supplies:** AP says Saudi stocks of high-end interceptors are under pressure.

## Editor's Insight

The cost asymmetry AP describes is the core problem: cheap, domestically made Houthi drones and missiles are being met by expensive interceptors. If that gap persists, it may favour the attacker over time, though that is a possibility rather than a certainty.

AP's analysis also notes that air power rarely succeeds without ground troops, and that anti-Houthi ground forces are divided. Combined with the Houthi demand that Riyadh lift its blockade, which AP says would be seen as a defeat, diplomatic options look limited. Egypt's involvement could widen the conflict, but its stated defensive framing suggests caution.

Finally, the attacks tie Yemen to the Iran war and Gulf energy security. A senior Saudi diplomat told AP that Iran is encouraging the escalation; Iran denies arming the Houthis.
