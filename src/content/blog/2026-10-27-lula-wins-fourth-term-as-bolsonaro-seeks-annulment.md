President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva won Brazil's 25 October runoff, defeating Flávio Bolsonaro with <mark>51.76% of the vote to 48.24%</mark>, according to figures from the Superior Electoral Court (TSE) reported by Latin Times. Flávio Bolsonaro said he would ask to annul the result after a last-minute extension of voting, though the Associated Press noted it is unclear what legal route he has. Our [earlier post on the count](/blog/2026-10-26-lula-pulls-ahead-in-brazil-runoff-count-against-flavio-bolsonaro/) described the night before a winner was declared.

## What happened

### How big was Lula's margin in the Brazil runoff?

Lula won by about 4.3 million votes out of roughly 123.8 million cast, Latin Times reported, citing the TSE. The Associated Press, via ABC News, put his total at <mark>more than 64 million votes against just under 60 million</mark> for Bolsonaro, a margin of nearly 4%. The TSE declared Lula the winner at about 21:47 Brasília time, and he told supporters in São Paulo that the country needs peace and unity.

![Bar chart of the runoff result: Lula 51.76%, Flávio Bolsonaro 48.24%](/images/2026-10-27-lula-wins-fourth-term-as-bolsonaro-seeks-annulment/result.svg)

Lula is the first candidate to win a runoff after finishing second in the first round, Latin Times reported. In the first round on 4 October, Bolsonaro had 47.03% and Lula 45.16%. At 80, Lula is also the oldest person elected head of state in Brazil's history, and his is a record fourth term. He is expected to take office on 5 January 2027.

![Bar chart comparing each candidate's vote share in the first round and the runoff](/images/2026-10-27-lula-wins-fourth-term-as-bolsonaro-seeks-annulment/swing.svg)

### Why Bolsonaro wants the result annulled

Voting was extended at the last minute. Latin Times reported that Supreme Court Justice Cristiano Zanin extended it nationwide to 19:00 after reports of voter access problems, and the TSE paused its count at 17:20 until voting closed. The AP said both campaigns had asked for more time: Lula cited transport problems in São Paulo and Salvador, and Bolsonaro cited heavy rain in the south. The order came minutes before the 17:00 closing time, so many polling stations heard late.

Flávio Bolsonaro said he would request an annulment. The AP wrote that <mark>a path to annul the results could be difficult</mark> because Brazil's electronic voting system has a reliable record and no significant fraud has ever been detected. Latin Times did not report whether he had conceded.

### How the world and Brazil's institutions reacted

TSE president Kassio Nunes, whom Jair Bolsonaro appointed to the Supreme Court, recognised Lula's victory, the AP reported. Conservative regional leaders who oppose Lula also recognised the result quickly. US President Donald Trump had not commented, according to the AP.

## Background

### Why was this runoff so tight?

Brazil is polarised between Lula and the Bolsonaro family. Jair Bolsonaro, the former president and Flávio's father, is serving a 27-year prison sentence for a coup attempt, according to the AP. He also contested his 2022 loss to Lula. The right did well on 4 October: Latin Times linked to a separate report that it won 49 Senate seats and control of Congress, and the AP said Bolsonaro's party will hold more seats than any other.

Lula trailed after the first round, but turnout changed the picture. The runoff drew 14.1 million more voters than the first round, the AP reported. The first round had set a record for abstentions, while the runoff had the fewest ever, and analysts estimated that <mark>three-quarters of the additional votes went to Lula</mark>.

![Key-figure card: Lula's winning margin of about 4.3 million votes](/images/2026-10-27-lula-wins-fourth-term-as-bolsonaro-seeks-annulment/margin-card.svg)

### The digital fight over voters

Lula's campaign, led online by André Janones, ran a WhatsApp channel called "Lula's Army" that gathered almost 11 million people, the AP reported. On 15 October Brazil's top electoral court ruled that Meta must ensure equal treatment of the two candidates, and the company said it had addressed the matter.

## Why it matters

### What Lula's win means for Latin America

Bloomberg, cited by Latin Times, framed the result as a check on a rightward shift across Latin America. Brazil is the region's largest country, so its direction affects trade, climate diplomacy and the balance between left- and right-leaning governments. The vote also came as Brazil faces what the AP described as <mark>polarisation, a possible fiscal crisis and a right-wing Congress</mark> that Lula must work with.

### Why the dispute over voting hours matters

A contested result is a test of institutions. Jair Bolsonaro also contested his 2022 loss, the AP noted. This time the electoral court president, a justice appointed by Jair Bolsonaro, accepted the outcome. That makes a legal reversal look unlikely, though the AP did not rule out a challenge.

## What to watch

- **Annulment request:** whether Flávio Bolsonaro files, with which court, and on what grounds. The extension was requested by both campaigns.
- **Concession:** whether he accepts the result. Latin Times had no report of a concession.
- **Markets:** Asian and European markets open on Monday, and the real's reaction is the first signal of how investors read a fourth Lula term.
- **Congress:** how Lula builds a working majority against a right-wing legislature.
- **Inauguration:** the swearing-in is expected on <mark>5 January 2027</mark>.

## Editor's Insight

The first-round lead for Flávio Bolsonaro was about two points, and it turned into a roughly 3.5-point loss in the runoff. The reported turnout figures suggest why: <mark>14.1 million extra voters, mostly for Lula</mark>, outweighed the right's first-round advantage. That is an interpretation of the AP's estimate, and it suggests the first round understated how many voters could still be mobilised.

The annulment threat is a different kind of risk. Because both camps asked for the extension, and because a justice appointed by Jair Bolsonaro recognised the win, a challenge may be politically useful for Flávio more than legally viable. It could keep his base mobilised through a Congress where the right is strong. Whether it does so is a possibility, not a forecast.

For Lula, the margin is clear, but the arithmetic in Congress is not in his favour. A fourth term at 80 would depend on bargaining with a legislature controlled by opponents, which may limit the fiscal and social agenda he campaigned on. Investors will likely watch how the new government handles the fiscal pressures the AP flagged.
