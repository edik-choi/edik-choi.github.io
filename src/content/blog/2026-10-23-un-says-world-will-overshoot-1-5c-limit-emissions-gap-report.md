The United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) said on 21 October that <mark>holding warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot is no longer possible</mark>. Its Emissions Gap Report 2026, titled "The Point of Return," says the best remaining path is a temporary overshoot to about 1.7–1.8°C, followed by a return to 1.5°C by 2100. The findings land less than three weeks before the COP31 climate summit in Turkey.

![Key-figure card: best-case peak warming of 1.7–1.8°C, with about a 50% chance of returning to 1.5°C by 2100](/images/2026-10-23-un-says-world-will-overshoot-1-5c-limit-emissions-gap-report/key-card.svg)

## What happened

### What does the Emissions Gap Report 2026 say about 1.5°C?

The report is the 17th annual assessment of the gap between countries' climate plans and what the Paris Agreement requires. According to Down To Earth, it states that "the chance of holding global warming to below 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels with no or limited overshoot is gone." The Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency (PBL), which contributed modelling, quoted researcher Michel den Elzen saying the target "is no longer possible."

The best-case "lowest possible overshoot" pathway has <mark>peak warming of about 1.7–1.8°C</mark> around mid-century and a roughly 50% chance of returning to 1.5°C or below by 2100. UN Secretary-General António Guterres said, as quoted by political.org, that "overshoot is not surrender" and that it can be kept as small and as short as possible.

### How deep would emissions have to fall?

Relative to 2025 levels, global greenhouse gas emissions would need to fall <mark>18% by 2030, 45% by 2035 and 69% by 2040</mark>, according to PBL's summary of the report. Current national plans point the other way: fully delivered, they would cut emissions by only 5–8% by 2030 and 11–15% by 2035.

![Bar chart comparing emission cuts needed by 2030, 2035 and 2040 with cuts pledged in national plans](/images/2026-10-23-un-says-world-will-overshoot-1-5c-limit-emissions-gap-report/cuts-needed.svg)

### Where are emissions now?

Global emissions were <mark>58.4 gigatonnes of CO₂ equivalent in 2025</mark>, 0.1 gigatonne lower than in 2024, Down To Earth reported. Fossil CO₂ kept rising, and the small overall dip came from a drop of about 0.5 gigatonnes in land-use emissions, partly because wildfires were less intense than in 2024. G20 members account for 76% of global emissions, and over the past decade 63 countries reduced emissions while 124 increased them.

## Background

### Why is warming heading above 1.5°C?

The Paris Agreement of 2015 aims to hold warming well below 2°C and pursue 1.5°C. The world first recorded a year above 1.5°C in 2024, according to Down To Earth. September 2026 was the second-hottest September on record, 1.63°C above pre-industrial levels, according to the same report, which cites Copernicus.

The outlook has also worsened since last year. Under current policies the report projects up to <mark>2.8°C of warming this century</mark>; full delivery of all national plans gives 2.6°C, and adding net-zero pledges gives 2.1°C. PBL says these figures are worse than last year's estimates, because of the US withdrawal from the Paris Agreement and revised historical emissions data. The earlier assessment projected 2.3–2.5°C if countries fully implemented their plans, according to political.org.

![Bar chart of projected warming by 2100: 2.8°C under current policies, 2.6°C with all national plans, 2.1°C with net-zero pledges](/images/2026-10-23-un-says-world-will-overshoot-1-5c-limit-emissions-gap-report/warming-paths.svg)

### What is carbon removal's role?

An overshoot pathway only works if CO₂ is pulled back out of the air. The report says it requires removing and permanently storing about 320 gigatonnes of CO₂ this century, roughly eight years of 2024 global CO₂ emissions. PBL adds a rule of thumb: <mark>each five years of CO₂ emissions at current levels adds about 0.1°C</mark> to peak warming.

## Why it matters

### What does overshoot mean for people?

UNEP says every additional fraction of a degree, and every additional year above 1.5°C, raises climate risks. The 1.5°C level has never been considered safe, Down To Earth notes; it was set as a guardrail. Because the report concerns the whole planet's temperature, its implications reach every country, though the effects will differ by region.

### Is there any good news?

There are some. Since Paris, renewable electricity capacity has grown three times faster than expected and solar PV seven times faster, PBL reports. Continuing those trends with added policy could <mark>triple renewable capacity by 2030</mark>. By 1 September, 124 countries had submitted new national targets covering 75% of global emissions, more than double the number counted for last year's report, according to political.org.

## What to watch

### What will the EU push for at COP31 in Antalya?

COP31 is scheduled for 9–20 November in Antalya, Turkey. According to an internal EU document seen by Reuters, the bloc wants a "mitigation package" that turns existing pledges into real-world cuts, and an extension of the UN "mitigation work programme" to 2040. The document, drafted by Ireland, which holds the rotating EU presidency, also says there is "no scope" for new climate funding commitments, a stance likely to put the EU at odds with poorer countries, Reuters reported.

![Timeline from 2024 to COP31: first year above 1.5°C, UN confirmation in September, EU draft on 12 October, UNEP report on 21 October, COP31 on 9–20 November](/images/2026-10-23-un-says-world-will-overshoot-1-5c-limit-emissions-gap-report/timeline.svg)

Reuters also reported that the US has pulled out of global climate negotiations and that COP30 in Brazil last year ended without stronger commitments. Large emerging economies and fossil fuel producers have resisted tougher action, fearing harm to growth.

## Editor's Insight

The report's most important shift is one of framing. For a decade, 1.5°C was a target to hit; UNEP now treats it as a level to return to. That changes what negotiators are arguing about: not whether to cross the line, but how high and for how long. The numbers in the post show why the distinction matters, since each five years of unchanged emissions adds about 0.1°C to the peak.

The gap between the required cuts and the pledged ones is the story's core tension. A 45% cut by 2035 against pledges of 11–15% is not a gap that incremental tightening of existing plans is likely to close, and the EU's own draft concedes there is no new money on offer. A possible consequence, which is analysis rather than fact, is that COP31 turns into a debate about implementation and finance rather than fresh targets, with the EU's mitigation work programme extension as the main concrete deliverable.

The renewable growth figures offer a counterweight. Technology deployment has outrun forecasts, and PBL says policy support could triple capacity by 2030. If that holds, the main variable may be less technology than whether the largest emitters, including those that have not yet peaked, align policy with it. That is a forecast, not a certainty.

That counterweight depends on supply chains that are becoming contested. China's [draft export curbs on 40 technologies](/blog/2026-10-17-china-proposes-export-curbs-on-40-technologies-including-ai/) include know-how for making polysilicon, a core solar input, so the fastest route to more renewable capacity also runs through a geopolitical chokepoint. The countries with the least finance and the most exposure to warming lose most if overshoot lasts longer. Producers that control clean-energy inputs gain leverage.
