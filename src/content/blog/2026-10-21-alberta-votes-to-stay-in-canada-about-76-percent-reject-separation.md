Albertans have rejected the idea of starting a legal process toward separation: early results from Elections Alberta show <mark>about 76% voted to remain in Canada</mark> in the October 19, 2026 referendum, with roughly 24% backing a future binding vote on leaving. Prime Minister Mark Carney called it a clear choice for unity, while separatist leaders said their movement is not going away. This article explains what the Alberta referendum result means and what could happen next.

## What happened

With more than 1.5 million votes counted, about 76% supported remaining in Canada and close to 24% backed beginning a process toward separation, NBC News reported, citing unofficial Elections Alberta figures. Global News also reported around 76% for "remain" in early results released late on Monday, October 19. Question 10 was counted first, and the province must finish its hand count within 48 hours. These are unofficial, provisional counts, so final figures may differ slightly.

The vote was not a direct referendum on independence. A "yes" to the second option would <mark>only have set Alberta on a path toward a future separation vote</mark>, NBC News noted.

### How turnout compared with 2023

Participation was high even before polling day. <mark>More than 1.35 million votes</mark> were cast in advance polls and by special mail-in ballots, against roughly 810,000 by those methods in the 2023 provincial election, according to Global News. Some 519,082 mail-in ballots arrived by the cut-off, a tenfold increase in special ballots over 2023. Early results were delayed by more than three hours because some polling places opened late, and NBC News said more than 100 stations extended their hours.

![Bar chart comparing advance and special votes in 2026 and 2023](/images/2026-10-21-alberta-votes-to-stay-in-canada-about-76-percent-reject-separation/advance-votes.svg)

### How the result compared with polls

Polls since the start of 2026 had consistently shown <mark>about 70% of Albertans</mark> wanting to stay in Canada, Global News reported. The early count came in a few points above that level, and the movement's support, at close to a quarter of voters, was in line with what pollsters had found. As covered in an [earlier post on the vote](/blog/2026-10-20-alberta-votes-on-separation-referendum-results-pending/), polls had also suggested that many supporters of a second referendum wanted to send a message to Ottawa.

## Background

Alberta holds the world's fourth-largest proven oil reserves and is a conservative stronghold, NBC News reported. Resentment toward Ottawa is long-standing but has not usually translated into strong support for independence: former Premier Jason Kenney said separatist parties traditionally won only 1% to 2% of the vote.

### Why Premier Smith called the vote

Danielle Smith, who opposes independence, called the referendum under pressure from separatists in her United Conservative Party, according to NBC News. Kenney said anger over COVID-19 restrictions helped mobilise activists who later joined the separatist movement. NBC News also reported that Donald Trump's trade war and his talk of making Canada the 51st U.S. state reportedly helped Carney gain support in Alberta.

## Why it matters

For Ottawa, the result removes an immediate threat to national unity. Carney said Albertans "chose Canada" and that the result would help accelerate trade diversification, infrastructure and energy development, NBC News reported. In Global News, he said Albertans "chose unity, to build together, and to take care of each other."

### What Smith said

Smith wrote that "Albertans have voted for a stronger Alberta in a united Canada," according to Global News. She had promised that a 50% plus one majority would carry the day, and she <mark>ruled out another referendum for the rest of her UCP mandate</mark>.

### Separatists say they will continue

Separatist leaders pointed to the quarter of voters who backed their side. Keith Wilson said "at least a quarter of Albertans feel that Alberta needs to be treated better," NBC News reported. Jeff Rath of the Alberta Prosperity Project said <mark>the movement is not going away</mark> and wants Smith removed as UCP leader; he has suggested the party could splinter, possibly creating a new pro-independence party. Global News added that Rath is pushing for a special UCP general meeting to test Smith's leadership. On the other side, pro-Canada campaigner Thomas Lukaszuk said: "Don't mess with Canada!"

## What to watch

- <mark>Official final results</mark> for Question 10, which the province must complete within 48 hours of the count.
- Results of the other nine questions on immigration and constitutional matters, which Global News said are expected by Wednesday.
- Smith's address to Albertans on Tuesday evening and what it says about the province's direction.
- Whether Rath's push for a special UCP meeting gains support inside the party.
- Whether Smith's pledge not to hold another referendum survives pressure from separatists.

## Editor's Insight

The margin is clear, and the most reliable reading is that a large majority of voters chose to stay. The result landed a few points above where polls had sat all year, which suggests that voters who were uncertain, or who wanted to express frustration, did not in the end move to the "leave" side. That is an interpretation, not a finding, but it fits Wilson's own framing: his stated claim is that a quarter of Albertans feel the province needs to be treated better, not that a quarter want to leave.

The more consequential question may be inside the governing party. Smith called the vote under pressure from separatists in her own party, and the people who wanted a different outcome are now looking at her leadership rather than at the ballot. Whether that produces a challenge, a party split or nothing at all is a possibility rather than a certainty. A lopsided result gives Smith an argument that the issue has been settled; it does not remove the pressure that brought the question to the ballot.

For Carney, the result is a political gain, but one tied to conditions. He framed it as a chance to speed up trade diversification, infrastructure and energy development, and the grievances behind the "quarter" are likely to be tested against how quickly those promises show results. The final figures, and the other nine answers due this week, will show how much of the vote was about independence and how much was about the way Alberta is governed from Ottawa.

Compared with the polls in our pre-vote report, which ranged from about 66% to 74% for remaining, the result came in higher, so the separatist camp ended with less than even its weaker polling suggested. Carney is the clearest winner, and the separatists the clearest losers on the ballot. Smith sits in between: she can say the question has been answered, but she now leads a party in which the losing side is still organised.
