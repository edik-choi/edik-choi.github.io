On Thursday, 15 October 2026, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said Kyiv was ending its restraint on attacking Russian oil refineries. <mark>"The block is out. We're done,"</mark> he told the Financial Times, according to Reuters. Hours later, a fire was reported at a refinery in Russia's Ryazan region, days after US President Donald Trump announced an energy ceasefire.

## What happened

Trump announced on Sunday, 11 October, that an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine was "effective immediately", writing: "Both parties have agreed. Do not break it!" Reuters reported that he gave no further details and that Moscow had not commented. Zelensky said Ukraine supported the idea but wanted details and <mark>"real guarantees"</mark>.

According to Reuters and NBC News, <mark>Ukraine paused its refinery strikes for three days</mark>. Kyiv says Russia kept attacking its energy system, including power stations, and that hundreds of thousands of people lost electricity. The Kremlin said Ukraine's energy strikes undermine US peace efforts, and Russian President Vladimir Putin said Ukrainian long-range attacks had caused "nothing particularly significant".

On 15 October, Ukraine hit a Rosneft-owned refinery in Ryazan, southeast of Moscow. Reuters says it accounts for <mark>nearly 5% of Russia's refinery throughput</mark>. Regional governor Pavel Malkov said a drone attack caused a fire, and Russia's Defence Ministry said Ukraine had attempted a massive attack on the facility.

Zelensky also said more than 850 Russian drones had filled Ukraine's skies since early Thursday, according to NBC News.

## Background

The dispute is tied to fuel prices. Reuters reports that diesel prices have hit record highs since the United States launched its war against Iran in February, and that Trump blames Ukraine's refinery campaign for part of the rise ahead of the US midterm elections, which are less than three weeks away. The war with Iran has also disrupted shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, as covered in an earlier post on [the fertiliser plan that stalled](/blog/2026-10-15-hormuz-fertiliser-plan-stalls-un-warns-of-food-crisis/).

On Friday, 9 October, Trump announced a diesel deal under which Russia would supply <mark>over 300,000 tonnes (about 2.25 million barrels) of diesel</mark>. Reuters said analysts consider that volume too small to lower prices sustainably, and Republican Representative Don Bacon called for more sanctions instead. On Saturday, Trump warned Zelensky to stop the refinery attacks and urged Ukrainians to choose a new leader. Zelensky replied that he would hold an election if Putin agreed to a 60-day total ceasefire, noting that Ukrainian law bans elections under martial law.

![Diagram of pressure between Washington, Kyiv and Moscow](/images/2026-10-16-ukraine-ends-energy-truce-and-resumes-refinery-strikes/actors.svg)

Zelensky has said Ukraine would stop hitting refineries only if Russia stops striking Ukrainian power infrastructure. He also told the Financial Times that a halt on energy strikes would require more US-made Patriot interceptors, which he called a "minimum". Reuters reports that Washington has halted weapons supplies to Ukraine, although European allies can still buy US arms for Kyiv.

## Why it matters

Reuters describes the dispute as the biggest rift between Kyiv and Washington since Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. Two issues add to it. First, the Financial Times reported that US military officials told Ukrainian counterparts: "if you will not stop [the energy strikes], we will block intelligence." Zelensky said several ballistic strikes on Kyiv came without the usual warning from partners. NBC News says the US later described the incident as a technical glitch, and Trump denies withholding intelligence.

Second, Ukrainian officials said military Starshield terminals, the defence version of Starlink, stopped working reliably on fast-moving drones this week. The defence ministry said they worked only up to 100 mph (160 kph). Zelensky called it a policy decision. Elon Musk said any limits are decided by the US military and the president. On Thursday, Ukraine's chargé d'affaires Denys Sienik said speeds had <mark>returned to previous levels for now</mark>, and Ukraine has not received an explanation from SpaceX or the US Defense Department.

The energy fight also has human costs. Reuters reported that a six-year-old girl was killed in a Russian strike on Zaporizhzhia on 10 October, and that drones struck two chemical tankers near Romania's Black Sea coast, leaving seven sailors missing. A NATO source told Reuters the drones came from Russia; Romania's president did not say who was responsible.

## What to watch

- **Energy talks:** whether Washington, Kyiv and Moscow agree on a mutual halt to energy strikes, which Zelensky says is possible only if Russia also stops.
- **US support:** whether the dispute over intelligence warnings and Starshield speeds is resolved, and whether the weapons freeze continues.
- **Diesel prices:** whether the Russian diesel deal and Ukrainian strikes change fuel prices before the US midterms.
- **Europe:** Zelensky is due at an EU summit on Friday, and EU ministers are discussing more sanctions on Russia and Ukraine's accession talks.
- **Winter:** military experts quoted by NBC News warn that losing US intelligence or satellite access could weaken Ukraine's position as winter approaches.

## Editor's Insight

The truce asked Kyiv to give up one of its few levers on Russia's economy in exchange for an unspecified promise. The Ryazan refinery alone handles nearly 5% of Russia's refinery throughput, and strikes on such sites impose a direct cost that sanctions take longer to deliver. Ukraine says that during its three-day pause Russia kept attacking power stations and hundreds of thousands of people lost electricity. From Kyiv's side, a ceasefire without the "real guarantees" Zelensky asked for meant restraint on one side only.

The pressure from Washington is driven largely by American pump prices. Diesel has hit record highs since the US war with Iran began in February, midterm elections are less than three weeks away, and Trump blames Ukraine's refinery campaign for part of the rise. The 300,000-tonne Russian diesel deal, which analysts say is too small to lower prices for long, shows how far domestic fuel politics now shapes US policy toward the war. Moscow is the clearest beneficiary: it gains if US pressure restrains strikes on its refineries, and it gains a diesel sale.

The week also exposed how much of Ukraine's defence depends on systems controlled by others. The reported threat to withhold intelligence, the ballistic strikes that came without the usual warning and the Starshield speed limits all point to the same vulnerability, whatever their causes turn out to be. With US weapons supplies halted, Europe's ability to buy American arms for Kyiv has become more important to Ukraine.

Zelensky's demand for Patriot interceptors as a "minimum" ties the energy question to air defence: restraint on refineries would be traded for protection of Ukraine's grid. If no such exchange is reached, both sides may keep escalating their attacks on energy, and civilians bear the cost, as the killing of a six-year-old in Zaporizhzhia and the drone strikes on tankers off Romania, a NATO member, already show.
