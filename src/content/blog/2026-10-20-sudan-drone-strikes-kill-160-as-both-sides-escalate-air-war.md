A wave of drone attacks across Sudan has killed <mark>more than 160 people</mark> since Friday 16 October 2026, according to medics and rights groups cited by Al Jazeera. The deadliest strike hit a transport hub near a market in Al-Dibab, West Kordofan, on Sunday evening, where residents and a medical source said 80 people were killed. This article explains what the latest Sudan drone attacks reportedly did, who has been blamed and why drones are driving civilian deaths in the war between the army and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF).

## What happened

### How many people died in the Sudan drone attacks?

Al Jazeera reported that more than 160 deaths were recorded in western regions since Friday, based on accounts from medics and rights groups. The figures are preliminary and come from local sources; they could change as bodies are counted and the wounded are treated. Both the RSF and government forces were blamed for strikes in different places.

### What happened in Al-Dibab, West Kordofan?

On Sunday evening, 18 October, a drone hit a transport hub near a market in Al-Dibab, where villagers had gathered to find rides home. Residents and medics said <mark>80 people were killed</mark>, according to Al Jazeera. A witness, Ahmed Abakar Wadi, told AFP he had helped bury 50 bodies, one of them his brother, and that the drone struck twice, ten minutes apart. Another 47 wounded people were taken to nearby Al-Mujlad, where a medical source told AFP that <mark>30 later died</mark>. AFP described it as one of the deadliest single strikes since the war began in April 2023.

West Kordofan is under RSF control. The RSF's self-declared administration, known as Tasis, and the monitoring group Emergency Lawyers blamed the army for the Al-Dibab attack. That accusation comes from a party to the war and from a rights group, and the army's account was not given in the sources reviewed.

### Other strikes since Friday

- **Blue Nile province (Friday):** An RSF drone hit a gathering of displaced people near the Ethiopian border, killing <mark>41 people</mark>, according to Emergency Lawyers, which called it a humanitarian tragedy.
- **Nyala, South Darfur (early Monday):** A drone struck the RSF stronghold, killing 17 people, including seven RSF officers, and wounding 23, according to medical and RSF sources.
- **Khartoum (Monday):** France 24 reported that a drone strike on a house in the Nasser Extension neighbourhood, east of the airport, killed four people, three of them children aged two to six. The UN said strikes hit several locations in the capital, including near the airport, and that some flights were cancelled.
- **Gadaref state (Sunday):** A military source said air defences intercepted several RSF drones aimed at the Atbara and Setit dams, about 420 km east of Khartoum. The RSF had not commented.
- **Abyei (Monday):** The UN said at least 19 people were injured in a busy market.

## Background

### Why is Sudan's war being fought with drones?

The war began in April 2023, when army chief Abdel Fattah al-Burhan and RSF leader Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as Hemedti, fell out over plans to fold the RSF into the army. The two had jointly seized power in a 2021 coup, the UN notes. In June, Hemedti was sentenced to death in absentia for war crimes, according to Al Jazeera.

Al Jazeera reported that rising drone use in recent months has increased civilian casualties. Drones let either side hit towns, markets and infrastructure far from the front line, including places such as Khartoum, Nyala and the dams in the east. No definitive death toll exists for the conflict. ACLED counts at least 59,000 killed, Al Jazeera said, while aid workers cited by AFP estimate more than 200,000.

### What is the humanitarian toll?

The UN says about <mark>13 million people have been displaced</mark>, and aid groups describe the crisis as the world's largest displacement and hunger emergency. Al Jazeera reported that 33.7 million people, including 17.3 million children, need food aid this year, and that the UN estimates 825,000 children face severe acute malnutrition. UN News put the number facing acute hunger at about 20 million and said the risk of famine is rising in parts of Darfur and Greater Kordofan. Sudan's humanitarian response plan is <mark>just over 43 per cent funded</mark>.

## Why it matters

Civilians bear the cost. Several of the strikes reported this week hit places where ordinary people gather: a market transport stop, a camp-like gathering of displaced people, a family home. UN News said weekend strikes in West Darfur, South Darfur and North Kordofan reportedly killed at least 19 people, including 10 children. The Sudanese Red Crescent says 25 of its volunteers have been killed on duty since the conflict began.

The attacks also show the war spreading beyond its earlier front lines. Strikes reaching Khartoum's airport area, which disrupted flights, and attempts on dams in Gadaref raise the risk to infrastructure that millions rely on for transport, power and water, although the sources do not report damage to the dams.

Finally, the response is stalled. UN spokesperson Stephane Dujarric said the UN is "alarmed by the continued deadly drone strikes across the country that are killing civilians." Rosemary DiCarlo, a UN under-secretary-general, urged outside actors to stop fuelling the violence and the warring parties to return to negotiations. On Friday the US urged the UN Security Council to push for a humanitarian truce and a wider arms embargo, but <mark>China and Russia opposed expanding the embargo</mark>, Al Jazeera reported.

## What to watch

- **Verified tolls:** Check whether medics, the UN or independent monitors confirm the Al-Dibab figures and clarify how many of the 47 wounded are counted among the 80.
- **Responsibility:** Whether any investigation attributes the Al-Dibab strike, given the conflicting claims.
- **Security Council action:** Whether a humanitarian truce or wider arms embargo advances despite the opposition of China and Russia.
- **Funding:** Whether donors close the gap in a response plan that is only about 43 per cent funded.
- **Kordofan and Darfur:** The army is pushing to roll back RSF gains in Kordofan, so further strikes there are a risk.

## Editor's Insight

The pattern in this week's reports is that drones have become the main way both sides project force into areas they cannot hold on the ground. The sources show the RSF blamed in Blue Nile and the army blamed in West Kordofan, which suggests neither side has a monopoly on this tactic. That makes a negotiated limit on drone use harder to design: any monitoring scheme would have to cover both camps.

The diplomatic picture points the same way. A US push for a truce and arms embargo, and China's and Russia's opposition to widening the embargo, is a split that could keep the Security Council from acting. This is an analytical reading, not a reported outcome, and the council could still agree narrower steps such as humanitarian access.

For civilians, the combination of a 43 per cent funded response plan and strikes on markets and displaced communities suggests the humanitarian situation could worsen even without a change in front lines. That is a possibility rather than a forecast: aid delivery and famine risk depend on access, funding and the course of fighting in Darfur and Kordofan.
