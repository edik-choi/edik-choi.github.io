Exit polls from Israel's 27 October election show <mark>neither Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc nor Gadi Eisenkot's opposition</mark> reaching the 61 seats needed to govern. Netanyahu's Likud is the largest party, while his opponents hold a slightly bigger bloc. Final counting is still under way, and real results often differ from exit polls.

![Bar chart of Channel 12 exit poll seats: Likud 24, Yashar 22, B'Yachad 12, Joint List 11](/images/2026-10-28-israel-election-exit-polls-show-no-majority-for-netanyahu-or-eisenkot/party-seats.svg)

## What happened

### What do the Israel election exit polls show?

According to The Times of Israel, the Channel 12 exit poll gave <mark>Likud 24 seats</mark> and Eisenkot's Yashar party 22. Naftali Bennett's B'Yachad was projected at 12 seats and the Joint List at 11. The paper reported that the Jewish-led parties opposing Netanyahu would hold 52 seats, his supporters 50, and the Arab-led parties, which also oppose him, <mark>a record 18</mark>.

The Jerusalem Post, which summarised exit polls from Channels 11, 12 and 13, reported Likud at 24–25 seats and Yashar at 21–23. In later polls at 1:00 a.m. the two main blocs were tied at 51 seats each, with Likud at 27 and Yashar at 22. The paper listed B'Yachad at 12–14, the Democrats at 9–10, Yisrael Beytenu at 7–9, Otzma Yehudit at 6–7, the Religious Zionist Party-Zehut at 6, Shas at 6 and United Torah Judaism at 6–7.

![Key-figure card: turnout reached 77.7% when polls closed, the highest since at least 1973](/images/2026-10-28-israel-election-exit-polls-show-no-majority-for-netanyahu-or-eisenkot/turnout-card.svg)

### How high was turnout?

The Jerusalem Post said turnout was <mark>77.7% when polls closed</mark>, the highest since at least 1973 and 7.1 points above 2022. Two small parties, Ofer Winter's People of Israel and Yoaz Hendel's The Reservists, were projected to fall below the 3.25% threshold. Votes for parties that miss the threshold are wasted, which can make it harder for Netanyahu's bloc to reach a majority.

### Why is a 61-seat majority out of reach?

The Knesset has 120 seats, so a government needs 61. Neither camp is close, according to The Times of Israel. Adding the 52 opposition seats to the 18 Arab-led seats would give 70, but the paper notes that Arab-led parties have generally not joined governing coalitions.

## Background

### Why was this election so closely watched?

Netanyahu's government was in office during the 7 October 2023 Hamas-led attacks and served a full term, according to The Jerusalem Post. He is Israel's longest-serving prime minister, with more than 19 years in office in total. The opposition has demanded he be held accountable for 7 October and that a state commission of inquiry be set up. Our [preview of the vote](/blog/2026-10-25-israel-votes-tuesday-as-netanyahu-eisenkot-race-stays-tight/) covers the draft dispute among ultra-Orthodox parties and the war with Iran that shaped the campaign.

### What have the leaders ruled out?

The Jerusalem Post reported that Eisenkot has rejected serving under Netanyahu, including in a rotation arrangement, and that other opposition leaders have also ruled out a Netanyahu-led coalition. Opposition leaders have largely ruled out a government backed by the Arab parties as well, with Democrats leader Yair Golan the exception. Eisenkot has said the leader of the largest opposition party should be its candidate for prime minister, but no agreement has been announced.

## Why it matters

### What happens if no one can form a government?

The Times of Israel notes that if no coalition forms, Israel could hold <mark>a repeat election</mark> with Netanyahu staying on as caretaker prime minister. It compares the situation to 2019–2022, when the country voted five times. The Jerusalem Post also raised the possibility of a minority government that wins a confidence vote without 61 seats, though opposition to relying on Arab parties could complicate that route.

### Why does it matter beyond Israel?

A long stalemate would leave decisions on the Iran war and the Gaza aftermath with a caretaker government. That is a risk, not a forecast: the sources we opened do not say what a caretaker government would decide.

## What to watch

- <mark>The final count</mark>, which could move the blocs by a seat or two, especially for parties near the 3.25% threshold.
- Whether Eisenkot or Bennett opens talks with any party that backed Netanyahu, despite the pledges above.
- Whether the Arab-led parties' record result changes the opposition's position on relying on them.
- The president's consultations and who receives the first mandate to form a government.
- Any signal of a second election, or of a minority government seeking a confidence vote.

## Editor's Insight

The most striking number is not Likud's lead but the size of the Arab-led bloc. At 18 seats, it is large enough that the opposition's arithmetic only works if a party outside the traditional coalition game becomes part of it. Opposition leaders have mostly said they will not do that, so the exit polls describe a map where <mark>both camps need someone they have refused</mark>. That is the structural reason a result that looks close can still be a deadlock.

The comparison the Times of Israel draws with 2019–2022 is worth taking seriously. In that period five elections produced few lasting majorities, and the cost was political drift rather than a single crisis. A repeat of that pattern would favour whoever benefits from a caretaker status quo. Netanyahu remains prime minister in the interim, so a stalemate is not neutral: it is an advantage for the incumbent, and a cost for an opposition whose coalition depends on holding together.

Record turnout of 77.7% also cuts both ways. High participation makes it harder for either side to say the result is a fluke, and it raises the legitimacy of whoever eventually forms a government. But it also suggests that voters were mobilised by the 7 October inquiry question and the Iran war, issues on which the camps have little room to compromise. The Times of Israel says neither camp has a clear path to a government; anything beyond that is a possibility, not a fact.

For other governments and markets, the practical effect is uncertainty. Our earlier coverage of [Iranian warnings to US bases](/blog/2026-10-23-iran-warns-us-bases-as-hormuz-tanker-attacks-hit-october-high/) shows how fragile regional security already is. A caretaker government in Jerusalem is less likely to make large strategic concessions, and less likely to be restrained by a coalition partner. Winners from a stalemate are those who prefer the status quo; the losers are voters who turned out in record numbers expecting a decision.
