Serbians voted on Sunday, 25 October, in a snap parliamentary election that pits the party of longtime leader Aleksandar Vucic against a list built by the student protest movement. Vucic resigned the presidency in September to run for prime minister, and <mark>polls show the two sides close</mark>. Turnout and results had not been reported when this post was written.

## What happened

Serbia's 6.4 million voters were choosing a 250-seat parliament, according to the Associated Press. Vucic's Serbian Progressive Party (SNS) has ruled for 14 years. Its main challenger is the Students' List, which fields about 250 mostly new candidates, among them professors, actors, farmers and a rapper, according to Reuters and Al Jazeera.

### What the polls say about Serbia's election

Polling is thin. The only poll Reuters cited for October, from New Serbian Political Thought on 20 October, put <mark>the students at 44.5% and the SNS at 42.7%</mark>. An Ipsos poll cited by Al Jazeera gave the SNS 47% and the students 40%. Reuters reports that betting odds favour Vucic.

![Bar chart: the Students' List at 44.5% and the SNS at 42.7% in the 20 October NSPM poll](/images/2026-10-25-serbia-votes-in-snap-election-as-students-challenge-vucic/poll-chart.svg)

### Concerns about a fair vote

AP reports that the SNS has been accused of irregularities, including pressuring and bribing voters and inflating voter lists, which the party denies. AP adds that the ruling party controls the election authorities and the judiciary, and that the EU and international monitors have voiced concern about the safety and fairness of the vote. According to AP, officials detained six people suspected of planning unrest on election day.

## Background

### Why Serbian students are protesting

In November 2024 a railway station canopy collapsed in Novi Sad, <mark>killing 16 people</mark>. Students blamed corruption for shoddy renovation work and blockaded the political science faculty in Belgrade to demand justice. Reuters reports that in late January 2025 Prime Minister Milos Vucevic resigned a day after tens of thousands blocked a major Belgrade junction, and that in March 2025 hundreds of thousands marched in what it called the biggest anti-government march in a generation.

![Timeline from the November 2024 canopy collapse to the 25 October 2026 election](/images/2026-10-25-serbia-votes-in-snap-election-as-students-challenge-vucic/timeline.svg)

The movement has no single leader. AP says decisions are taken by voting in faculty assemblies known as plenums. Its list is headed by cardiologist Ilija Srdanovic, though Reuters notes the students say he would not automatically become prime minister. Its symbol is a red handprint.

### How Vucic has held power

Vucic has dominated Serbian politics since 2014, first as prime minister and then as president, according to Reuters. Under him the economy has seen strong growth and falling unemployment, the agency says. Critics accuse him of democratic backsliding and of silencing critics and the media; rights groups and activists told Reuters that police have beaten protesters. Vucic and the authorities deny the corruption and violence allegations. He says foreign agents in Croatia and Montenegro fund the students, who deny it.

## Why it matters

Al Jazeera notes that a Vucic defeat would be a major political shift, comparable to Viktor Orban losing power in Hungary in April. Mario Bikarski, a senior Europe analyst at Verisk Maplecroft, called the vote <mark>"a watershed moment for the country"</mark>, according to Reuters.

![Diagram: the Serbian Progressive Party and the Students' List compete for 250 seats](/images/2026-10-25-serbia-votes-in-snap-election-as-students-challenge-vucic/actors.svg)

### What the vote means for Serbia and the EU

Vucic seeks closer ties with China, a major investor, and with Russia, Serbia's traditional ally and main gas supplier, while keeping EU membership open, per Reuters. Accession talks are stalled, and the EU has criticised the lack of judicial and anti-corruption reform. The students have said little about EU accession or relations with Kosovo, and pro-European critics say they would not sanction Russia. Consultancy Teneo warned that "strong bureaucratic resistance could generate short-term institutional instability" under a student-led government.

## What to watch

- **Results and turnout:** Whether either side claims victory on election night. AP reports that analysts fear both could, and that Vucic might not concede if he loses.
- **Security:** Both sides have spoken of fears of violence; <mark>six people were detained</mark> before the vote, according to AP.
- **Coalition talks:** Whether the SNS or the Students' List can assemble a majority in the 250-seat chamber, and who the students would name as prime minister.
- **Observer reports:** Findings from international monitors on the conduct of the vote.

## Editor's Insight

The close race matters less for who wins than for what the protest movement has already changed. By turning a grassroots, leaderless campaign into a ballot list, the students moved the contest from the street to institutions, where Vucic's control of the election authorities and judiciary, as AP describes it, is an advantage. Forecasts are possibilities, but a narrow result would likely be contested in either direction.

The foreign-policy picture is also uncertain. Vucic has balanced Beijing, Moscow and Brussels. The students' silence on Russia, Kosovo and EU entry means a change of government would not automatically mean a clear turn toward the EU. Observers such as Teneo suggest the first challenge could be governing at all.

Finally, the comparison drawn with Hungary shows how electoral outcomes in Central and Eastern Europe are being read as tests of whether long-dominant parties can be voted out. Serbia's result could be cited in that debate, though the two countries differ in size, EU status and party systems.
