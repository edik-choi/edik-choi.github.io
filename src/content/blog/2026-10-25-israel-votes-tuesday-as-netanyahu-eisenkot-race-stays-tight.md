Israelis vote on Tuesday, 27 October, in <mark>the first general election since the Gaza war began</mark>, with polls showing Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing bloc and former army chief Gadi Eisenkot's bloc neck and neck. Thousands rallied against the government in Tel Aviv on Saturday, three days before the vote. The result could shape how Israel handles the war with Iran, the draft of ultra-Orthodox men and the demand for an inquiry into 7 October 2023.

![Key-figure card: 61 of the Knesset's 120 seats are needed for a majority, and May polls showed neither camp reaching it](/images/2026-10-25-israel-votes-tuesday-as-netanyahu-eisenkot-race-stays-tight/key-card.svg)

## What happened

### What is at stake in the Israel election on 27 October?

According to Al Jazeera, the vote on <mark>Tuesday, 27 October 2026</mark> is the first general election in Israel since the war on Gaza began. On Saturday, 24 October, thousands gathered in Hostages Square in Tel Aviv, and other anti-government demonstrations took place elsewhere in the country, per Haaretz as quoted by the outlet. The rally was organised by the October Council, founded by families of captives taken to Gaza three years ago, which demands accountability for the failure to prevent the 7 October 2023 Hamas attack and a <mark>state commission of inquiry</mark>.

Opposition figures attended, including Gadi Eisenkot, leader of the Yashar party, and Yair Golan of the Democrats. Eisenkot was greeted with the chant "Who's coming? The next prime minister," according to The Times of Israel as cited by Al Jazeera. The October Council warned police about possible "provocations" by pro-government agitators.

### How close is the race?

Al Jazeera reports that Netanyahu's right-wing bloc and Eisenkot's bloc are <mark>neck and neck</mark>, and that neither is projected to win a parliamentary majority. Netanyahu has been prime minister since late 2022; Eisenkot is described as his main centre-right rival.

### What is the inquiry dispute?

The government rejects an independent commission and offers a "political" one, with half its members appointed by the government and half by opposition legislators. Al Jazeera says opposition leaders will boycott it.

## Background

### Why is Israel holding an election now?

The immediate trigger was a coalition crisis. The Irish Times reported on 13 May that the ultra-Orthodox United Torah Judaism party had <mark>quit Netanyahu's government</mark>, and that the coalition pulled all bills from the Knesset agenda, fearing it had lost its majority. The Haredi parties want a law making the decades-old military exemption for yeshiva students permanent. Likud promised in December 2022 to prioritise it, but it was never passed. Heavy demands on reservists from the Gaza war and later conflicts made extending the exemption politically difficult, the paper said.

On 24 May, The Times of Israel reported that lawmakers had unanimously approved a preliminary reading of a bill to dissolve the Knesset, and that elections had to be held by 27 October in any case. Netanyahu reportedly told the Haredi parties the coalition lacked a majority for the draft bill and suggested waiting until after the elections.

### What is the Haredi draft dispute?

According to the Times of Israel, citing the army, <mark>over 79,000 conscription orders</mark> have been issued since the High Court's 2024 ruling, yet only 2,100 of those men have enlisted. About 32,000 people are classified as draft evaders and another 50,000 have received formal warnings. The article says the number of evaders could reach 80,000 to 90,000.

### How did the Iran war enter the campaign?

Al Jazeera says the US-Israeli war with Iran began on 28 February, when Iran's then Supreme Leader was assassinated in the opening strikes; his son Mojtaba Khamenei succeeded him. An interim deal to end the war has unravelled, and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian blamed Washington for it on 24 October. US President Donald Trump said he "may do another little Epic Fury." Netanyahu says he remains committed to overthrowing Iran's government, while Eisenkot is seen as less inclined to resume strikes. Our earlier report on [Iranian warnings to US bases](/blog/2026-10-23-iran-warns-us-bases-as-hormuz-tanker-attacks-hit-october-high/) covers the regional tension.

## Why it matters

### What could the result change?

The outcome could decide <mark>whether Israel resumes strikes on Iran</mark>, with consequences for Gulf shipping and energy markets already under strain. Al Jazeera notes that leading Israeli parties largely agree in opposing an independent Palestinian state, so a change of government may shift tone and military policy more than that position.

### Why do the numbers matter?

A majority needs 61 of 120 seats. The Irish Times reported in May that polls gave the coalition about 50 seats, Jewish opposition parties close to 60 and the two Arab parties about 10. Those figures are months old, and Al Jazeera's latest description is simply that neither bloc is projected to reach a majority, so <mark>coalition bargaining after the vote</mark> is likely to matter as much as the result itself.

## What to watch

- **Seat count:** whether either bloc gets near 61 seats or the result is another stalemate.
- **Coalition talks:** whether the Haredi parties, who warned in May that their automatic support for Netanyahu will not hold after the election, back him again.
- **Inquiry and draft:** whether a state commission of inquiry or a draft law is on the next government's agenda.
- **Iran:** whether fighting resumes before or after the vote, and how the new government responds.
- **United States:** US midterm elections follow on 3 November, per Al Jazeera.

## Editor's Insight

The campaign shows a country voting on three questions at once: who led it through 7 October, who will serve in its army, and whether the war with Iran continues. Those questions cut across the usual left-right split. The Haredi parties helped bring down the coalition they once anchored, and the October Council's rally frames security failure as a matter of accountability rather than ideology.

Polls this close tend to make small parties and post-election bargaining decisive. If the picture in the sources holds, neither Netanyahu nor Eisenkot can count on a clean majority, and a weeks-long negotiation is a possibility, not a forecast. Eisenkot would then face the same dependence on the Haredi and Arab parties that has complicated Netanyahu's position.

For outside observers, the main link is Iran. A government more reluctant to strike could ease pressure on Hormuz shipping and on energy prices, while a hawkish one could prolong it. That is an inference from the candidates' reported positions, not a prediction.

If no bloc wins a majority, the main winners would be the small parties that can make or break a coalition, the Haredi and Arab parties among them, because both leaders would need their votes. The main loser could be whoever forms a government, since that leader would start out dependent on partners whose demands, from army service to the course of the war, pull in different directions.
