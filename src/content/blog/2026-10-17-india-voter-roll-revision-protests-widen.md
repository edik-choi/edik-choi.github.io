Protests have spread across India over a nationwide revision of voter lists, with opposition parties and civil society groups demanding that <mark>Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar resign</mark>. On 16 October 2026, the Election Commission announced two changes, according to The Hans India, while it and the government continue to deny any wrongdoing.

## What happened

The dispute centres on the Special Intensive Revision (SIR), a revision of electoral rolls in which voters must provide documents showing they are eligible to stay on the lists. According to NBC News, protesters want Kumar to resign and want the revision to end. They also want an inquiry into his conduct and a more independent process for appointing election commissioners.

The Election Commission says the exercise removes duplicate and ineligible entries. Critics say it can exclude legitimate voters, especially poorer and more vulnerable people who lack older records. Opposition parties allege the changes would remove voters likely to back them and benefit Prime Minister Narendra Modi's BJP. The BJP says the update aims to weed out "infiltrators," NBC News reports.

![Key figure card: about 130 million names reported removed from India's voter rolls](/images/2026-10-17-india-voter-roll-revision-protests-widen/key-figure.svg)

The scale is large. The Hans India reports that the SIR has led to <mark>over 13 crore (130 million) names</mark> being cancelled from the rolls. In an opinion piece for TIME, the legal scholar Gautam Bhatia gives the same figure. The numbers come from press accounts rather than a single official tally, and the Commission describes the entries as duplicates and ineligible names.

In West Bengal, the revision removed about 9 million names, roughly 12% of the electorate, before state elections earlier this year, according to NBC News. The BJP won the state for the first time. NBC News adds that Commission data indicates <mark>some regional voter lists could shrink by as much as 30%</mark>.

![Bar chart: about 12% of West Bengal's voter list removed versus up to 30% on some regional lists](/images/2026-10-17-india-voter-roll-revision-protests-widen/west-bengal-share.svg)

Street action has grown. A youth campaign known as the "Cockroach" movement has helped lead the protests, NBC News reports. Its earlier street protests forced the education minister to resign in July. Reports from the past week describe opposition leaders marching toward the Supreme Court, where the chief justice noted that the issues were already pending before the court.

Bhatia writes in TIME that on one Saturday in October, New Delhi saw more than 20,000 police and paramilitary personnel deployed, internet access suspended, and <mark>over 7,000 people detained</mark>, including opposition leaders, lawyers and journalists. That account is from an opinion piece, and this post has not confirmed the figure with a news report.

On 16 October the Commission announced two changes, according to The Hans India. First, the Special Summary Revision, a routine update that adds eligible voters and had been suspended during the SIR, will resume in 17 states and Union Territories with a qualifying date of 1 January 2027. Second, recognised political parties will receive draft and final electoral rolls as searchable PDF files instead of image-only files. The same report notes that reopening the summary revision does not automatically restore names removed during the SIR.

## Background

The SIR began last year in the state of Bihar and has since expanded to most states and federally administered territories, NBC News reports. Civil society groups challenged the exercise in the Supreme Court in June 2025, according to TIME. Bhatia writes that in summer 2026 the Court upheld the exercise and set up tribunals to hear appeals, saying the data did not show "systemic disenfranchisement."

![Timeline from the start of the revision in 2025 to the Commission's 16 October announcement](/images/2026-10-17-india-voter-roll-revision-protests-widen/timeline.svg)

Bhatia also writes that about 90% of decided appeals from excluded voters in West Bengal ended with restoration to the rolls. Critics cited in his piece raise further concerns, including bulk challenges to voters' entries and algorithm-driven exclusions. These are the author's assertions, not findings by the Commission or the courts.

An internal dispute has added to the pressure. NBC News reports that The Indian Express said in September that two of the three commissioners formally objected to several decisions on voter list revisions, registration procedures and digital systems, and that Kumar rejected the objections. The Commission described the situation as "differing views and deliberations." It has also said its decisions were taken in accordance with the law.

## Why it matters

India's electorate numbers in the hundreds of millions, so any change to how voters are listed affects one of the world's largest democratic exercises. If the removals are largely duplicates and ineligible entries, as the Commission says, the revision cleans up the rolls. If eligible voters are left off, as critics fear, it could <mark>shape the outcome of future elections</mark>. The evidence cited so far points both ways: appeals have restored many names in West Bengal, according to Bhatia, but excluded voters with pending appeals could not vote in the state election.

The independence of the Election Commission is also at stake. It is a constitutional body, and NBC News notes that changes require a majority vote of its members. Reports of disagreement among commissioners have made the question of who decides, and how, part of the public argument.

The dispute has also become a test of how India handles street protest. TIME's opinion piece describes a heavy security response in the capital, while the government's position, as reported, is that there has been no manipulation.

## What to watch

- <mark>State elections early next year</mark>, which NBC News says could intensify the dispute as the revision expands. The Hans India lists Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Punjab, Goa and Manipur among the states with assembly elections next year.
- Whether the resumed Special Summary Revision lets people who were removed rejoin the rolls, given that it does not automatically restore names.
- Whether the Supreme Court takes up the pending issues again, and how the appeals tribunals perform.
- Whether the Commission responds to the call to change how commissioners are appointed, or to reports of disagreement among its members.
- Further demonstrations and the government's response to them.
