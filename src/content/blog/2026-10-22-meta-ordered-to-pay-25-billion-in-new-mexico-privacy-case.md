A New Mexico state judge has ordered Meta Platforms to pay <mark>$25 billion</mark> after a jury found that the company misled consumers about how it handled their data. Al Jazeera described it as the largest fine ever imposed on Meta. The company says it will appeal. This article explains why Meta was ordered to pay $25 billion in New Mexico and what could happen next.

## What happened

On Wednesday, October 21, 2026, state District Judge Francis Mathew ordered Meta to pay <mark>$25 billion</mark>, NBC News and Al Jazeera reported. NBC News said the judge found New Mexico law supported a penalty of more than $219 billion but reduced it to $25 billion. The judge wrote that Facebook's wrongdoing, which he described as systematic and lasting for years, "goes to the heart of" the company's core business practices. According to Al Jazeera, he also said: "Facebook promised users that they (the users) controlled their data. This was false."

![Key figure card showing the $25 billion penalty ordered against Meta](/images/2026-10-22-meta-ordered-to-pay-25-billion-in-new-mexico-privacy-case/penalty-card.svg)

### What the jury found in September

The penalty follows a verdict on September 25. Jurors found that <mark>26 of 29 statements</mark> by Meta and its leaders about privacy and content moderation were misleading, NBC News reported. They found more than <mark>43 million violations</mark> of New Mexico's consumer protection law, counted by the number of users or state residents who saw each misleading statement. State law allows up to $5,000 per violation, which is how the judge arrived at a ceiling above $219 billion.

![Key figure card showing 43 million violations and 26 of 29 statements judged misleading](/images/2026-10-22-meta-ordered-to-pay-25-billion-in-new-mexico-privacy-case/jury-card.svg)

### How big is the $25 billion penalty compared with earlier fines

At an October 1 hearing, the state's lawyer, Randi McGinn, asked for $35 billion to $40 billion, while Meta urged the judge to cap penalties at $3.45 billion. The amount ordered sits between the two. For comparison, the Federal Trade Commission imposed a <mark>$5 billion penalty</mark> on the company in 2019 over the same Cambridge Analytica scandal, according to NBC News. New Mexico Attorney General Raúl Torrez called the result one of the largest, if not the largest, single-state verdicts against Meta in US history.

![Bar chart comparing the state's request, the amount ordered, the 2019 FTC fine and Meta's proposed cap](/images/2026-10-22-meta-ordered-to-pay-25-billion-in-new-mexico-privacy-case/penalty-figures.svg)

### Where the money would go

Under state law all proceeds go to New Mexico's school system, NBC News reported. Torrez said the money will fund the state's education system. Al Jazeera noted that its planned appeal could reduce the amount.

## Background

### Why New Mexico sued Meta over Cambridge Analytica

Torrez sued Meta in 2021, after reports that Cambridge Analytica harvested data from as many as <mark>87 million Facebook users</mark> through a third-party app. The defunct British consultancy had worked for Donald Trump's 2016 presidential campaign and obtained the data without user consent from a researcher who had been allowed to deploy an app on Facebook. The state alleged Meta lied to New Mexico users about who could access their data and how it handled hate speech and misinformation. It also alleged the company tolerated harmful content when that helped its bottom line.

### Other New Mexico cases against Meta

This is not the first New Mexico ruling against the company this year. In March 2026, a Santa Fe jury found Meta misled users about the safety of young people on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp and ordered $375 million in civil penalties. In a later phase, a judge ordered Meta to pay $567 million into a state fund for teen mental health and to put protections in place for teen users. Separately, NBC News reported that six other states settled their Cambridge Analytica claims for $459.2 million as part of a $17.1 billion multistate settlement Meta reached in August, which addressed claims that its platforms harmed teen mental health.

## Why it matters

### What Meta argues

Meta's lawyer, Matt Nicholson, called a sum as high as $40 billion "shocking to the conscience" and argued that jurors found no evidence that any New Mexican saw the statements or was harmed, according to Al Jazeera. The company has also said a penalty in the billions risked violating its constitutional protections against excessive fines and due process, and that it does not sell user data. Chief legal officer CJ Mahoney said Meta expects to <mark>win on appeal</mark> and argued the lawsuit amounts to a tax on speech.

### What analysts say about the financial impact

Gil Luria of D.A. Davidson told NBC News the ruling was costly but unlikely to affect Meta's value, noting the company is worth nearly $2 trillion and would likely keep appealing. Daryl Lim of Penn State Dickinson Law said the size of the penalty does not automatically mean it would be overturned, given how New Mexico's law is written. Experts expect a lengthy appeal, so the practical financial effect may not be known for some time.

### Why other regulators will pay attention

Because the judge counted violations by the number of people who saw each statement, the case offers a template for how a state consumer protection law can scale a penalty with audience size. Other states and regulators may study that approach, though whether it survives appeal is not yet known.

## What to watch

- **The appeal:** Meta has said it will appeal. Whether a higher court cuts the amount or the constitutional arguments about excessive fines succeed will decide how much is eventually paid.
- **Remaining cases:** The earlier New Mexico youth-safety penalties and remedies are part of the wider legal pressure on the company.
- **Copycat actions:** Watch whether other state attorneys general bring similar per-violation claims.
- **Market reaction:** Analysts quoted by NBC News expect little effect on Meta's value, but investor reaction in the coming days will show whether that holds.

## Editor's Insight

The headline figure matters less than the method. The judge did not set the penalty by estimating harm to individual people; the jury's count of more than 43 million violations was multiplied by a statutory maximum, producing a theoretical ceiling above $219 billion. That arithmetic gives state prosecutors a lever that scales with a platform's reach, which is why a company worth nearly $2 trillion still has a strong incentive to fight it.

The size of the reduction, from more than $219 billion to $25 billion, also suggests that courts are wary of treating the statutory maximum as the right answer. Meta's argument that no individual was shown to be misled is the kind of point appellate courts often weigh. It is possible, though not certain, that the amount will change on appeal; the experts quoted by NBC News expect the process to be long.

For the wider technology industry, the case sits alongside the earlier New Mexico youth-safety verdicts and the multistate settlement in August. Taken together, they point to US states, not just federal regulators, becoming the main source of financial risk for platforms over what they tell users about data and safety. Whether that trend continues will depend on how higher courts treat per-violation penalties of this scale.
