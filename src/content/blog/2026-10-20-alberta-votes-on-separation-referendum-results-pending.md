Voters in the Canadian province of Alberta cast ballots on October 19, 2026 in a referendum that includes a question on whether the province should <mark>begin the legal process toward a binding separation referendum</mark>. At the time of writing, in the early hours of October 20, no official result had been published. This article explains what the Alberta separation referendum asks, what polls showed beforehand and what a result either way would set in motion.

## What happened

Polls were open from 9 a.m. to 8 p.m. Mountain Time on October 19, according to Global News. The ballot carries 10 questions; the separation question is Question 10 and is counted first, with early results expected after the polls closed and a final count in the early hours of October 20. The other nine questions concern immigration and possible changes to Canada's Constitution, and their results are expected by Wednesday.

### How many Albertans voted early

Turnout before voting day was unusually high. Elections Alberta reported <mark>1,356,873 preliminary votes</mark> through advance and special ballots between October 13 and 17, compared with 809,988 at the same point in the 2023 provincial election, Global News reported. With 3,148,343 eligible electors, about 43% had voted in the advance period, versus 27.5% in 2023. Elections Alberta expects overall turnout of 80 to 85 per cent.

![Chart comparing advance votes in 2026 and 2023 in Alberta](/images/2026-10-20-alberta-votes-on-separation-referendum-results-pending/advance-voting.svg)

Some voting locations extended their hours after late openings, which delayed results, Global News noted. Ballots are counted by hand.

### What the separation question asks

The question offers two choices: remain a province of Canada, or have the government begin the legal process for a binding provincial referendum on separation. A "leave" majority would therefore <mark>not separate Alberta by itself</mark>; it would authorize a second, binding vote.

## Background

The referendum question was added on May 21, 2026, after a court ruling struck down an earlier separatist petition. Separatist campaigners and Premier Danielle Smith, who opposes separation, have framed the vote in sharply different terms.

### Why the process went through the courts

On May 13, 2026, Alberta Justice Shaina Leonard ruled in favour of several First Nations, finding the referendum process unconstitutional because First Nations were not consulted. A question that incorporates the "Alberta Forever Canada" petition was then added to the ballot on May 21, according to Wikipedia's summary of CBC and Politico reporting.

![Timeline of the Alberta referendum from the May 2026 court ruling to the October 19 vote](/images/2026-10-20-alberta-votes-on-separation-referendum-results-pending/timeline.svg)

### Where polls stood before voting day

Pollsters consistently found most Albertans preferring to stay. Abacus Data found <mark>74% of decided voters favoured remaining</mark> and 26% wanted the process to begin, Fox News reported. Ipsos put the remain side at about 70%, with support for beginning the process at 23%, up three points from September, per Global News. Angus Reid Institute's poll published October 16 showed 66% for remaining and 34% for a future binding referendum.

![Bar chart of three pre-vote polls showing majority support for remaining in Canada](/images/2026-10-20-alberta-votes-on-separation-referendum-results-pending/poll-comparison.svg)

## Why it matters

Even a clear "remain" result may not close the issue. In the Angus Reid poll, <mark>62% of Albertans said the separatist movement will never end</mark>, and Abacus found 61% expect another vote on separation even if most choose to stay, according to Fox News.

### A message to Ottawa, or a path out?

Angus Reid found that among supporters of a future binding referendum, 60% said their position was about sending a message to Ottawa while 37% said it was about separation. Separatist organiser Cory Morgan told Fox News he believes about one in three Albertans want out, and that his side looks to Quebec's bargaining power as a model.

![Bar chart showing 60 percent send a message to Ottawa versus 37 percent separation](/images/2026-10-20-alberta-votes-on-separation-referendum-results-pending/message-to-ottawa.svg)

### Federal and outside pressure

Prime Minister Mark Carney told a rally of more than 5,000 people in Edmonton that the referendum comes at the worst possible time, indirectly linking separatist pressure to outside efforts to divide Canada, Fox News reported. The Globe and Mail reported that separatist leaders met Republican strategists in Calgary in January, a claim summarized by Fox News and Wikipedia; these reports have not been independently confirmed here. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent called Alberta a natural partner for the United States.

### Consequences for the governing party

Morgan said <mark>over half of the United Conservative Party's roughly 55,000 members support independence</mark>. Angus Reid said a separatist party could "probably spell the death of the UCP". Smith, who opposes separation, told CBC News she would not hold another referendum while leading the province if most Albertans vote to stay. The next general election is scheduled for October 18, 2027.

## What to watch

- <mark>Official Question 10 results</mark> and the margin, which will signal whether a second vote is politically plausible.
- Whether Smith's pledge not to call another referendum holds under pressure from within the UCP.
- Any legal challenge: the Constitution has no provision for unilateral provincial secession, per the Wikipedia summary, so a "leave" win would meet constitutional and federal-law hurdles.
- Results of the nine immigration and constitutional questions, expected by Wednesday.
- Ottawa's response and any Carney statement after the count.

## Editor's Insight

The pre-vote data points to a result that is clear on the headline question but ambiguous on what it means. If polls of roughly 66% to 74% for remaining prove accurate, the separation option would lose by a wide margin. Yet the same polls show that a large share of Albertans, including many who back a future referendum, see the vote as a way to <mark>send a message to Ottawa</mark> rather than a wish to leave. That suggests grievance, rather than a settled desire for independence, may be what drives the movement, though this is an interpretation, not a finding.

The sharp rise in early voting is a second signal. A turnout of 43% before election day, against 27.5% in 2023, indicates unusual engagement; whether that benefits either side cannot be inferred from the numbers alone. It does mean a result will carry more legitimacy than a low-turnout vote would.

Finally, the political risk may sit inside Alberta's governing party rather than in the ballot count. If Morgan's estimate of UCP sympathy is even partly right, a lopsided "remain" result might not end pressure on Smith. A possibility, not a certainty, is that the question returns through party politics ahead of the 2027 election. Readers should treat this article as a pre-result snapshot and check Elections Alberta for the official figures.
