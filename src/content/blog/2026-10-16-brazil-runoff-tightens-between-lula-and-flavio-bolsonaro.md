Brazil's presidential race is heading into its final stretch with <mark>two new polls showing Senator Flavio Bolsonaro two points ahead</mark> of President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva. The surveys, released on 15 October by Quaest and Datafolha, put the right-wing challenger at 46% and 47%, against 44% and 45% for Lula, according to Al Jazeera. <mark>The run-off is on 25 October</mark>, and many experts consider it too close to call.

## What happened

Quaest, in its first poll since the first round, found Flavio Bolsonaro at 46% and Lula at 44%. Datafolha had Bolsonaro at 47% and Lula at 45%. In Datafolha's previous survey Bolsonaro had 49%, so <mark>his lead shrank from four points to two</mark>. Both leads sit within the two-percentage-point margin of error, according to Al Jazeera.

![Bar chart of Datafolha run-off polls on 25 September, 6-7 October and 15 October, showing the lead moving from Lula to Bolsonaro and narrowing](/images/2026-10-16-brazil-runoff-tightens-between-lula-and-flavio-bolsonaro/runoff-polls.svg)

The first round was held on 4 October. Neither candidate cleared the more than 50% of valid votes needed to win outright. With nearly 99% of votes counted, <mark>Bolsonaro had about 47% of valid votes and Lula 44.9%</mark>, according to Al Jazeera. Electoral court figures put Bolsonaro at about 56 million votes and Lula at almost 53 million. NPR, using figures with more than 99% counted, reported roughly 47% and about 45%.

Most pre-election polls had expected Lula to finish first. A Datafolha survey released on 25 September, for example, had him at 40% to Bolsonaro's 36% in the first round and ahead 47% to 45% in a run-off scenario.

Since then, Lula has held large rallies that have drawn millions of people, Al Jazeera reported. Analysts say Bolsonaro has not gained as much as expected from the elimination of other conservative candidates. Ronaldo Caiado and Renan Santos, who together won about 5.3 million votes, have said they will not endorse either candidate in the run-off.

![Bar chart of first-round votes in millions: Bolsonaro about 56, Lula almost 53, Caiado and Santos about 5.3 combined](/images/2026-10-16-brazil-runoff-tightens-between-lula-and-flavio-bolsonaro/first-round-votes.svg)

## Background

Lula, 80, is seeking a non-consecutive fourth term. He narrowly defeated Jair Bolsonaro, Flavio's father and a former president, in 2022. <mark>Jair Bolsonaro is serving a 27-year sentence</mark> for plotting to overturn that result and prevent Lula from taking office; NPR reported that he is held under house arrest. Flavio Bolsonaro, 45, is a senator and the eldest son. He has said he will carry on his father's legacy, and according to NPR he has pledged to cut the deficit and realign foreign policy with Washington.

The first-round result surprised pollsters. Analysts told Reuters, as cited by Al Jazeera, that Bolsonaro may have benefited from "protest votes", which could limit his growth in the run-off. Lula called the result "unexpected" and said: "The game is about to start. We are in extra time."

![Timeline from the 25 September poll through the 4 October first round and 15 October polls to the 25 October run-off](/images/2026-10-16-brazil-runoff-tightens-between-lula-and-flavio-bolsonaro/campaign-timeline.svg)

Voters have said they are concerned about crime and the cost of living, and Brazil's relationship with the United States has become tense. Lula has accused the United States of trying to meddle in the election, and the US State Department has denied it, according to NPR. Bolsonaro rallies have featured American flags, NPR reported.

On 4 October the right also made significant gains in Congress. Al Jazeera reported that Bolsonaro's Liberal Party (PL) was on track to become the largest force in the Chamber of Deputies and was gaining seats in the Senate. Michelle Bolsonaro, Flavio's stepmother, won a Senate seat in the Federal District, and Carlos Bolsonaro, his brother, was elected senator in Santa Catarina.

## Why it matters

Brazil is the largest country in Latin America, and the first round alone drew <mark>roughly 109 million votes for the two leading candidates</mark>, based on the electoral court figures above. The run-off decides who leads the country for the next term, with direct effects on social spending, fiscal policy and relations with Washington.

The two candidates offer different directions. Lula has pledged to continue broad social spending and to stand up to US policies toward Latin America, according to NPR. Bolsonaro is courting business and investor interests and has won endorsements from right and centre-right parties, Al Jazeera reported.

Lula's government has also made recent moves aimed at voters. The Senate approved a constitutional amendment <mark>cutting the maximum workweek from 44 to 40 hours</mark> without a pay cut, which Lula called "a historic achievement for millions of workers." The government has also banned sports betting, linking it to rising gambling addiction and household debt, and plans a constitutional amendment for free public transport in all cities on weekends.

A stronger right-wing Congress could make it harder for Lula to advance his agenda if he wins another term, Al Jazeera noted. The reverse also holds: NPR noted that the congressional gains could strengthen Flavio Bolsonaro's base if he wins.

## What to watch

- **Final polls:** Further Datafolha and Quaest surveys before 25 October will show whether the <mark>narrowing of Bolsonaro's lead</mark> continues or reverses.
- **Endorsements:** Caiado and Santos have declined to back either candidate, so their voters' choices are a swing factor.
- **Campaign security:** NPR noted that the 2022 run-off saw a sharp rise in political violence, citing the Brazilian news site Pública.
- **US-Brazil relations:** Accusations of foreign interference and the candidates' differing stances toward Washington may shape the closing days.
- **Result and transition:** The run-off is on 25 October, and the winner takes over a Congress where the right made gains.

## Editor's Insight

The first round is the best reason to treat these polls with caution. Most pre-election surveys expected Lula to finish first; a Datafolha poll on 25 September had him ahead 40% to 36%, yet Flavio Bolsonaro came out on top. Both run-off leads now sit inside the margin of error, so the published numbers say little more than that the race is close.

The arithmetic of the eliminated candidates matters more than the polls. Bolsonaro finished the first round about 3 million votes ahead, with roughly 56 million to Lula's almost 53 million. Ronaldo Caiado and Renan Santos, who together won about 5.3 million votes, have declined to endorse anyone. That uncommitted bloc is larger than the gap between the two leaders, and analysts' suggestion that part of Bolsonaro's vote was a protest vote points to why it may not move to him automatically.

Whoever wins, the Bolsonaro family has already gained. The Liberal Party is on track to be the largest force in the Chamber of Deputies, and Michelle and Carlos Bolsonaro won Senate seats. A Lula victory would therefore mean governing against a stronger right-wing Congress, while a Flavio Bolsonaro victory would give the right both branches. The congressional result has reduced the room for Lula's agenda even before the run-off is decided.

The vote is also a verdict on 2022. Lula narrowly beat Jair Bolsonaro that year, and the former president is now serving 27 years for plotting to overturn the result. A close outcome would test again whether the losing side accepts the count. Abroad, the stakes are about alignment: a Flavio Bolsonaro government has pledged to realign with Washington, while Lula has accused the United States of meddling, so the result will shape how Latin America's largest country positions itself.
