On Saturday, 17 October 2026, <mark>more than 550,000 people marched across France</mark>, according to the Interior Ministry, in protests over rising living costs that grew out of weeks of student demonstrations. Violence broke out in several cities, and separate cost-of-living protests were held the same day in Belgium, Italy and Portugal.

## What happened

The Interior Ministry counted about 550,000 demonstrators nationwide, including 110,000 in Paris, according to Al Jazeera. The CGT union put turnout at 800,000, Euronews reported.

![Bar chart comparing the Interior Ministry count of 550,000 with the CGT union count of 800,000 for France on 17 October 2026](/images/2026-10-18-france-cost-of-living-protests-spread-across-europe/turnout.svg)

Police used tear gas and water cannon against masked protesters in Paris, where several parked cars were set on fire. Protesters broke into the town halls of Marseille and Lille, and a tourist office was set ablaze in Lyon. In Limoges, a fire set by masked protesters <mark>largely destroyed the town hall</mark>, a building more than 140 years old, Al Jazeera reported. Dozens of shops were looted in several cities.

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said late on Saturday that <mark>about 1,200 people had been arrested</mark> and <mark>150 police officers injured</mark>. An earlier preliminary Interior Ministry count had been about 400 arrests, Euronews reported. Euronews also cited AFP as saying a protester in Saint-Étienne lost a hand to a police crowd-control grenade, according to a police source.

![Two key-figure cards: 1,200 people arrested and 150 police officers hurt, as stated by the French prime minister](/images/2026-10-18-france-cost-of-living-protests-spread-across-europe/toll.svg)

President Emmanuel Macron wrote on X that there is "no room for violence in our Republic" and that those who attacked prefectures, businesses and town halls "will be prosecuted and punished." Lecornu blamed ultra-left groups for orchestrating the violence, with delinquents joining in. Paris Mayor Emmanuel Grégoire said that "entire streets have been devastated."

## Background

The protests began in mid-September at a high school in Créteil, a Paris suburb, when students demanded education reform, citing dilapidated buildings, overcrowded classrooms and teacher shortages, according to Al Jazeera. On 5 October a 15-year-old lost a hand to a police stun grenade at a protest in Lens, Euronews reported.

![Timeline from mid-September student protests in Créteil to the nationwide protest day on 17 October](/images/2026-10-18-france-cost-of-living-protests-spread-across-europe/timeline.svg)

The movement then widened to cover failing public services, fuel prices and living costs. The CGT union called for higher wages and pensions and lower prices. Participants included nurses, teachers, farmers, retirees and remnants of the "yellow vest" movement of 2018, Al Jazeera reported.

The government is also under financial strain. Al Jazeera reported that it has struggled to reduce public debt and reassure investors, and that <mark>its borrowing costs are at their highest in more than 20 years</mark>. Similar debt concerns featured in the IMF's recent [forecast warning on AI and debt](/blog/2026-10-14-imf-raises-2026-growth-forecast-warns-on-ai-and-debt/).

## Why it matters

The unrest was not confined to France. According to Al Jazeera, <mark>hundreds of thousands of Europeans</mark> joined separate, uncoordinated demonstrations in four countries.

- **Belgium:** Police used tear gas and water cannon in Brussels, and water cannon in Liège. The protests followed weeks of student demonstrations against university registration fees.
- **Italy:** Peaceful rallies took place in Milan and Bologna alongside clashes in which protesters threw stones. Italian media reported "several" injuries and arrests, without figures.
- **Portugal:** Tens of thousands marched in Lisbon against the cost of living, low wages and housing costs. Al Jazeera reported that house prices have more than doubled since 2017. Government minister Carlos Abreu Amorim said the government "will not yield to shouts from the street."

![Four boxes summarising protests in France, Belgium, Italy and Portugal on 17 October 2026](/images/2026-10-18-france-cost-of-living-protests-spread-across-europe/countries.svg)

Alberto Alemanno, a professor of EU law and policy at HEC Paris, described France as the "epicentre of this movement". He said the protests are several separate movements with "shared grievances", including underfunded education, rising living costs and uncertain prospects for graduates. The unrest comes in the final stretch of Macron's presidency, before elections in about six months, according to Euronews and Al Jazeera.

## What to watch

- **Further mobilisation:** Alemanno said early-stage movements are forming in Greece, Germany and Austria, with mobilisation expected "in the coming week".
- **Official figures:** Arrest and injury counts changed during the day, and turnout estimates differ between the government and unions. Final tallies may differ from those reported on Saturday.
- **Government response:** Whether Paris offers concessions on wages, education or prices, or pursues prosecutions of those involved in the violence, as Macron has pledged.
- **Elections:** Alemanno noted that national elections are due in Spain, France, Italy and Poland within 12 months.
- **Public finances:** Lecornu said limited public funds cannot keep covering the damage, while the government's borrowing costs remain high.

## Editor's Insight

The obvious comparison is the "yellow vest" movement of 2018, whose remnants joined Saturday's marches. That movement began over fuel prices and spread into a broad revolt against living costs. This one began in a Créteil high school over crumbling classrooms and widened into the same terrain of fuel, wages and pensions. Both share a structure that makes them hard to settle: no single organiser, many grievances, and no one at the table who can accept a deal on behalf of the street.

The government's room to respond is narrower than in 2018. Its borrowing costs are at their highest in more than 20 years, and it has struggled to reassure investors about public debt. The protesters' demands for higher wages and pensions and lower prices collide directly with that constraint. It is the same tension the IMF described: governments are urged to cut spending while households absorb an energy shock.

The fuel costs at the heart of the protests are being felt well beyond France. They are the same prices that forced a [Spanish rescue charity to postpone its Mediterranean mission](/blog/2026-10-15-spanish-rescue-charity-delays-mediterranean-mission-over-fuel-costs/). The four countries that saw protests on 17 October were not coordinated, but Alemanno's description of "shared grievances" fits a continent where the same price shock lands on different local failures, from university fees in Belgium to housing in Portugal.

The political cost falls mainly on incumbents. With elections about six months away and Macron's presidency in its final stretch, the government is likely to be blamed both for the grievances and for the disorder. Its response so far, prosecutions and blaming the ultra-left, and Portugal's refusal to "yield to shouts from the street", suggest governments are choosing firmness over concessions. That may hold order in the short term, but it leaves the grievances themselves for the next government.
